# Reference B: Card Anatomy

## B §1 — Universal Card Elements

Every card in every format requires these elements. There are no exceptions for any print-and-play, digital, or physical card:

**Image:** Minimum 50% of card face. For resource cards (land, energy), can be 100% image. For physical TCGs, the image is the primary recognition tool at table distance. Art quality signals product quality.

**Faction/type colour:** A colour-coded visual indicator distinguishing faction, card type, or rarity. Gwent uses border colour for rarity. Magic uses the card body colour for faction. Yu-Gi-Oh! uses card background for type. The system must be consistent across the entire card pool. When introducing new factions or types, new colours must be distinct from existing ones.

**Cost indicator:** The resource expenditure required to play the card. Must be immediately visible, typically in a corner. Can use symbols, numbers, or both. If the game has no resource cost (e.g. single-card-per-turn designs), this slot is repurposed for something else (level, provision value, etc.).

**Stat values:** For cards that can attack/defend. Must be visually prominent — typically larger than resource cost. Position in corners for fast reference. If a card has multiple attack values (e.g. different attack types), all must be visible without reference to another card.

**Card title:** The name. Font/colour can indicate rarity tier or foil status. Title must be unique within the game.

**Text box:** Serves two purposes:
1. Rules text — the card's mechanical behaviour, including keywords and exceptions
2. Flavour text — narrative/lore content, written in italics by convention

**Legal information:** Card number in set, illustrator credit, copyright. Small text, lower margin.

**Back face:** Identical across all cards in the game. Any variation on the back face must be intentional and format-specific (e.g. double-faced cards). A unique back face is a privacy leak that lets opponents track card position.

## B §2 — Card Anatomy for Digital CCGs

Digital card games relax some physical constraints and introduce others:

**Relaxed:** Physical size limits do not apply. Art can be animated. Rules text can overflow to a pop-up tooltip (though primary keywords should still appear on-card). Costs can use dynamic UI indicators rather than printed symbols.

**New constraints:** Information hierarchy must survive small screen sizes (mobile). Cards must be readable at thumbnail size in the opponent's hand indicator. Loading/animation time must not impede fast gameplay. Rules text that requires scrolling creates cognitive friction — keep it short.

**UI anatomy difference:** Digital cards often use a secondary information panel triggered on hover/tap. This lets the card face carry cleaner art while full rules text is accessible. Physical TCGs cannot do this — all information must live on the card face.

## B §3 — Card Type Taxonomy

**Persistent cards (stay on field until removed):**
- Creature/unit — attacks, defends, has combat stats
- Enchantment/aura — modifies a creature; removed when creature is removed
- Field spell/effect — modifies the entire playing field while active
- Resource (if physical) — land cards, energy cards; typically not removed unless by specific effects

**Non-persistent cards (resolved and discarded):**
- Spell/instant — immediate single effect
- Trap/reaction — triggered when opponent performs an action
- Equipment — attaches to a creature, persists until creature is removed or effect removes it

**Advanced card types (introduced in expansions):**
- Fusion/synchro — constructed from multiple field cards meeting specific conditions
- Token — generated by card effects, not drawn from deck
- Double-faced — different rules on each face; flip under specific conditions

**Rules for new card types:** Never introduce new card types mid-game without a format break or dedicated expansion. New types must come with a complete rules clarification and must be retested against the entire existing card pool before release. Introduce one new type at a time, never two simultaneously.

## B §4 — Keyword Design Principles

Keywords are shorthand for mechanics. Their value:
- Reduce text box length
- Create faction/set identity
- Enable fast recognition in play
- Allow combo interactions through shared vocabulary

**Keyword creation rules:**
1. A keyword must do one thing. Multi-part keywords create ambiguity.
2. Keyword names should reflect function intuitively. "Flying" means something. "Aetheric" means nothing until learned.
3. Every keyword must have a written definition in the game's glossary. The card text is the shorthand; the glossary is the authority.
4. Keywords introduced in a set should remain in that set's context. Faction-exclusive keywords should not appear on neutral cards without explicit design intent.
5. Every new keyword must be tested against every existing keyword for degenerate interactions before publication.
6. When two keywords interact in an undefined way, the rules must resolve it explicitly. Do not leave interactions to player adjudication — this causes arguments.

**Keyword interaction audit:**
Before any keyword is finalised, answer:
- What happens when [this keyword] meets [existing keyword X]?
- What happens when two cards with [this keyword] fight each other?
- Can this keyword be applied to itself or stacked?
- Is there an existing counter-keyword, and should one be created?

## B §5 — Rarity Design

Rarity tiers must serve a design purpose, not just a commercial one.

**Two rarity philosophies:**

**Rarity = Power (problematic):** Rarer cards have higher stats or cheaper costs for equivalent stats. This is simple to design and terrible for balance. It creates a direct pay-to-win structure. The gap between players who have all rares and those who do not becomes insurmountable.

**Rarity = Complexity (preferred):** Rarer cards have more mechanics, more conditional interactions, and harder-to-execute utility. They are stronger when used correctly but unreliable when misused. Common cards remain viable for skilled players. This is harder to design and better for the health of the game.

**Practical rarity design:**
- Common: one to two keywords maximum; stats near benchmark for cost
- Uncommon: two to three keywords; slight deviation from benchmark; introduces some faction interaction
- Rare: complex keyword interaction or strong utility effect; requires understanding of the meta to use well
- Legendary/Mythic: single copy per deck; high impact; high conditional complexity; risks backfiring; possible alternate win condition or game-state reset

**Visual differentiation:** Foils, holographic treatments, alternate art, higher-quality card stock. These are cosmetic. They must never affect gameplay. Cosmetic rarity can coexist with gameplay rarity but must be clearly distinguished.

## B §6 — Symbology

Symbol systems reduce text box length and increase visual comprehension speed.

Design rules:
- One symbol = one meaning, always. No symbol is used for two purposes.
- Symbols must work at small print sizes. Test at 6-point equivalent scale.
- Symbols should be interpretable by new players after brief explanation, not requiring a manual each time.
- Symbols are investment. Once printed, they cannot be changed without a full reprint. Establish your symbol grammar before manufacturing begins.
- If a game grows beyond 8–10 symbols, consider whether some can be collapsed into one symbol with a numerical modifier.

## B §7 — Card Design Template (Starter Checklist)

Before any card is finalised:
- [ ] Does the card have a unique title?
- [ ] Is the cost clearly indicated and correctly calculated for the card's power level?
- [ ] Are all stats present and correctly positioned?
- [ ] Is the text box complete, unambiguous, and using standardised terminology?
- [ ] Are all keywords defined in the glossary?
- [ ] Has the card been tested against every existing keyword for degenerate interaction?
- [ ] Does the card's rarity reflect its complexity, not just its power?
- [ ] Does the art direction brief describe the image, faction colour, and any mandatory visual elements?
- [ ] Is the flavour text consistent with the game's setting?
- [ ] Does the card serve the thesis mechanism of the game?

## B §8 — Art Direction Brief

Every card needs an art direction brief before artwork is commissioned. A brief without direction produces art that misses the faction identity, scale, or emotional register needed.

**Mandatory brief elements:**
- **Subject:** What or who is depicted. Be specific: "a heavily armoured undead knight mid-charge, mounted on a skeletal horse" not "an undead warrior"
- **Faction visual language:** The faction's colour palette, design motifs, and aesthetic register. Reference existing approved art in the set.
- **Scale and framing:** Is this a close portrait, a medium action shot, or a wide establishing image? Card art reads better at medium to close framing.
- **Mood and tone:** Aggressive, mournful, triumphant, foreboding, comedic. This must match the card's mechanical identity. A sacrifice card should not look joyful.
- **Action or state:** Is the subject in action (dynamic, energetic art) or in a stable state (character portrait, establishing shot)?
- **Mandatory exclusions:** Elements that must NOT appear. "No human faces visible — faction wears masks" or "no modern technology visible in this fantasy setting."

**Brief length:** A good brief is one paragraph (5–8 sentences). A two-sentence brief leaves too much to interpretation. A two-page brief constrains the artist unnecessarily. Medium specificity is the target.

## B §9 — Card Cycle Design

A cycle is a set of cards with parallel designs across multiple factions. Each card in the cycle occupies the same structural position in its faction but expresses that position with faction-specific mechanics.

**Example cycle structure:**
- Faction A, 3-cost creature: "Gains +2/+0 when attacking" — aggressive faction's 3-drop
- Faction B, 3-cost creature: "Gains +0/+2 when defending" — defensive faction's 3-drop
- Faction C, 3-cost creature: "Draws a card when played" — value faction's 3-drop
- Faction D, 3-cost creature: "Deals 2 damage to a random enemy when played" — aggro-control faction's 3-drop

**Cycle benefits:**
- Provides immediate structural completeness — one card per faction at a given cost slot
- Creates cross-faction comparison that helps players understand faction identity
- Simplifies set architecture planning — a cycle guarantees coverage
- Creates collector appeal — players collect the full cycle

**Cycle design rules:**
- Every card in the cycle must be approximately equal in power at its benchmark cost — cycles that have one clearly better member destroy the faction balance they're meant to illustrate
- Cycle keywords or effects should be thematically coherent across the cycle — the parallel structure should feel intentional, not accidental
- A set should have no more than two or three named cycles, or the set loses coherent identity and becomes a collection of parallel structures

## B §10 — Card Interaction Worked Example

The richest design moments in TCGs/CCGs come from cards that individually seem limited but combine to produce unexpected outcomes. A teaching example using three cards:

**Ember Strike** (Spell, Common, 2 mana): Deals 5 fire damage to the opponent.

**Elemental Ward** (Field Spell, Uncommon, 3 mana to play + 2 mana upkeep): While active, all elemental damage directed at the controlling player is blocked.

**Arcane Feeder** (Creature, Uncommon, 4 mana, 2/3): Whenever any spell is played while this creature is on the field, deal 2 damage to the opponent.

In isolation: Ember Strike ends games quickly, Elemental Ward is a conditional defence, Arcane Feeder is a 4-cost 2/3 which is below benchmark (a 4-cost should be closer to 4/4). In combination: the opponent uses Elemental Ward to survive Ember Strike. But Arcane Feeder's trigger fires on the spell play regardless of whether the spell succeeds — dealing 2 damage that bypasses the Ward entirely.

**Design lessons from this example:**
- Understated cards earn their place through keyword interactions, not raw stats
- "While X is on the field, Y happens" triggers must specify what counts as the trigger — the spell being played or the spell succeeding
- Multi-card synergies are where depth lives; designing cards in deliberate interaction clusters produces more rewarding play than designing each card in isolation
- Every card should have at least one intentional combo it is part of, with all other interactions audited for unintended ones

## B §11 — Copy Limits and Draw Probability

The probability of drawing a specific card is a function of deck size and copy count. These numbers matter for balance because strategies that depend on drawing a specific card are only reliable if that card's probability is high enough to be consistent.

**Reference table:**
- 60-card deck, 1 copy: ~12% chance in opening 7 cards
- 60-card deck, 2 copies: ~22% chance in opening 7 cards  
- 60-card deck, 4 copies: ~40% chance in opening 7 cards
- 40-card deck, 3 copies: ~40% chance in opening 7 cards
- 40-card deck, 2 copies: ~28% chance in opening 7 cards

**Rarity-tiered copy limits (common design rule):** Four copies of any common card; two copies of any rare card; one copy of any legendary card. Designers do not have to follow this exactly, but the principle is sound: the more powerful the card (rarity as proxy for power/complexity), the fewer copies a player can run, reducing the reliability of drawing it.

**Why unlimited copies is broken:** If any card can be run at 60 copies in a 60-card deck, a player can guarantee drawing that card every turn. Any card that is good enough to run at maximum becomes a solved strategy.

**Deck size and randomness trade-off:** Smaller deck = less randomness = easier to draw specific cards = shallower strategic decisions. The 12-card deck in Marvel Snap means almost every strategy can be built with exactly the cards intended — but this caps deck variety significantly. The 60-card deck creates genuine uncertainty about draws and rewards deck construction skill. There is no right deck size; choose based on how much variance you want the game to contain.

## B §12 — Extended Card Type Library

Beyond the basic persistent/non-persistent taxonomy in B §3, these card types appear across CCG/TCG designs and require definition when used:

**Location/Field:** A card that modifies the play area or creates a special zone while in play. Affects all cards in a defined space. Must define: how many can be in play simultaneously, who controls it, whether opponents can interact with it, and what happens when it is removed.

**Relic/Artifact:** Equipment without a required host creature. Sits in its own zone, provides a continuous effect, and may have an activated ability. Requires a separate zone and rules for how many can be in play at once.

**Mission/Quest:** A card with a completion condition rather than an immediate effect. Sits face-up in a mission zone. When the condition is met, the mission resolves for a reward. Must define: where it sits, whether it is public information, whether opponents can interfere, and what happens if the condition becomes impossible.

**Objective:** Similar to Mission but typically tied to a scoring or win-condition contribution rather than a reward. Used in points-scoring game structures. Must define: how points are awarded and when.

**Leader/Commander:** A card with special status that defines constraints on the rest of the deck (faction identity, keyword access, resource style). May begin outside the deck. Rules must define: what happens when a Leader is defeated, how often they can re-enter play, and whether they can be targeted like normal cards.

**Campaign Card:** Used in campaign/narrative expansions. Carries persistent state between sessions (damage taken, experience gained, items collected). Not used in competitive formats. Requires a storage mechanism for between-session state.

**Design rule for new card types:** Introduce one new card type per expansion maximum. Each new type requires its own zone, its own phase rules (when can it be played), its own interaction rules with all existing types, and its own glossary entry. Do not introduce a new card type unless it enables a strategy that existing types cannot support.
