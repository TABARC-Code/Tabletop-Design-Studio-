# Reference G: Expansion Design

## G §1 — What Expansions Are For

Expansions are not just more cards. They serve structural purposes:

1. **Revenue:** The primary commercial function. Without expansions, a TCG/CCG has no sustainable income after the base set.
2. **Meta disruption:** New cards shake up dominant strategies, preventing any one archetype from being permanently best.
3. **Narrative advancement:** The game's world moves forward. New factions, events, and characters give collectors a reason to engage beyond competitive play.
4. **Mechanical evolution:** New card types, keywords, and mechanics deepen the game without requiring a rules reset.
5. **Balance correction:** Some expansions include deliberate counter-cards for dominant strategies, acting as a soft balance intervention.

An expansion that does only one of these things is weaker than one that does three simultaneously.

## G §2 — Set Philosophy

Every expansion should have a central design statement — a theme, mechanical focus, or strategic intent.

**What a good set philosophy enables:**
- Designers have a filter for which cards belong in the set
- Players have a reason to buy the set even if they're not competitive (thematic resonance)
- Marketing has a clear message
- Cards feel cohesive rather than miscellaneous

**Examples of set philosophies:**
- "This set introduces tribal synergies — cards that become stronger when more cards of the same creature type are in play"
- "This set is about the faction war reaching a flashpoint — new dual-faction cards for the first time"
- "This set focuses on the graveyard — mechanics that make discarded cards matter more than ever"

**What to avoid:** A set with no philosophy is a bag of random cards. These sets have lower engagement, weaker narrative identity, and are harder to balance because cards don't interact with each other thematically.

## G §3 — Set Architecture

**Minimum viable set:**
- Enough cards to support new deck archetypes (not just single card additions to existing decks)
- Coverage across all rarity tiers
- Cards for each active faction
- At least one neutral card that supports multiple factions
- If introducing new keywords: a glossary update and tutorial content

**Large set:**
- New mechanic or card type that adds a new dimension to the game
- New faction or major faction expansion
- Cross-faction cards or mechanics that reward multi-faction play
- Themed cycle of cards (one version per faction with parallel design)

**Rarity distribution (guideline, adjust per design):**
- Common: 40–50% of set
- Uncommon: 30–35% of set
- Rare: 15–20% of set
- Legendary/Mythic: 5–8% of set

Shifting this distribution changes the feel of collecting. More commons means players can build competitive decks more cheaply. Fewer commons (more rares) increases pack value for collectors but raises the competitive entry cost.

## G §4 — Power Creep Prevention in Expansion Design

The most dangerous expansion mistake is unconscious power scaling. Designers naturally want new cards to feel exciting. "Exciting" defaults to "more powerful." This is the origin of power creep.

**Anti-creep practices:**
- Benchmark every new card against the F §1 power curve before finalising it
- For every new card that exceeds the benchmark, identify the condition or cost that justifies it
- Design "utility upgrades" to old strategies rather than "power upgrades": a new card that lets players execute an old strategy more consistently is less creepy than one that executes a new strategy that makes the old one obsolete
- Rotate sets: making older cards non-legal in Standard formats allows power to increase in absolute terms without destroying historical cards' viability in Legacy formats

## G §5 — Introducing New Card Types

New card types are among the highest-risk expansion decisions. See B §3 for taxonomy.

**Pre-release checklist for new card types:**
- [ ] Does the new type fill a strategic gap that existing types cannot fill?
- [ ] Has it been tested against every keyword in the existing pool?
- [ ] Does it have a clearly defined phase in which it can be played?
- [ ] Does it have a clearly defined interaction with the graveyard and remove-from-game zone?
- [ ] Does it have a clearly defined maximum number per deck (if applicable)?
- [ ] Has it been tested in both aggressive and defensive deck contexts?
- [ ] Does its introduction create any cards in the existing pool that become degenerate in combination?

If any item fails, delay the type until the next expansion. Releasing a new type that requires immediate errata damages trust.

## G §6 — Digital vs Physical Expansion Differences

**Physical expansions:**
- Fixed print runs; once printed, errata requires a new printing or official rules override
- Booster packs; distribution is random within rarity distribution
- Lead time of 6–12 months from design to retail
- Cannot be easily updated after release

**Digital expansions:**
- Can be patched after release (see F §9)
- Can introduce individual cards between major sets (Marvel Snap model)
- Monetisation structures more flexible (timed exclusives, bundles, season pass)
- Can include animated card variants with no gameplay effect
- Can run limited-time events that introduce experimental card mechanics

**Hybrid:** Many physical TCGs now have digital CCG counterparts. Design both simultaneously where possible. A card that works in both mediums is more commercially valuable. A card that only works in one medium should be format-flagged clearly.

## G §7 — Adventure/Story Expansions (Digital CCGs)

Digital CCGs can present expansions as narrative content: solo battles, story-driven scenarios, guaranteed card rewards for completion.

This approach:
- Gives non-competitive players a reason to engage with the expansion
- Provides a controlled context for introducing new mechanics (tutorial-by-design)
- Guarantees at least some cards to players who complete the story, reducing the purely luck-based acquisition

Design note: story expansion content should be canonically consistent with the ongoing game world. Players who care about lore will notice inconsistency. Adventure content is also an opportunity to explain new mechanics through guided play rather than rulebook reading.

## G §8 — Release Events and Launch Products

A set's release should be treated as an event, not just a product drop.

**Prerelease events:** Players receive sealed product (a specified number of packs) before the official release date and build and play decks from what they open. Prereleases serve:
- Community building — players play with strangers around a shared new experience
- Feedback collection — last-chance player feedback before competitive season begins with new cards
- Product seeding — each prerelease attendee leaves with cards from the new set, becoming a community advocate

**Prerelease kit requirements:** Fixed set of packs (enough to build a functional sealed deck); promotional card not available in retail packs (creates attendance incentive); score sheet or event structure for organised play.

**Launch weekend:** The weekend of official retail availability. Events at game shops, promotional pricing on starter kits, buy-a-box promotional cards. The launch window is the highest-visibility period for player acquisition — new-player-friendly events in this window have outsized long-term value.

**Convention demo products:** Separate from retail. A convention demo kit should contain a simplified version of the game that can be learned and played in 15 minutes. Not the full game — a curated subset that demonstrates the core decision and creates desire for the full experience. Convention demos are brand awareness tools, not revenue products.

## G §9 — Promo Cards

Promo cards are special versions of existing cards (alternate art, special foil treatment) or exclusive cards not available in retail packs.

**Types of promo cards:**
- **Tournament promo:** Awarded to participants or top finishers in organised play events. Creates incentive for tournament participation. Should be either a cosmetic variant of a playable card (keeps competitive relevance) or a card of only moderate power (avoids pay-to-win from exclusive tournament access).
- **Buy-a-box promo:** Included with purchase of a booster box from a retailer. Rewards retail purchase. Should be a useful but not format-defining card.
- **Judge/staff promo:** Given to tournament officials and event staff. Typically higher-quality production than player promos. Recognises community service.
- **Convention exclusive:** Limited print run at a specific event. Creates scarcity and collector value. Never be the only source of a competitively mandatory card — convention exclusives should be cosmetic variants only.

**Promo card design rule:** A promo card's gameplay effect must be achievable through normal acquisition (pack purchase, wildcard crafting) even if the promo art or foil treatment is exclusive. Exclusive gameplay effects on promos are pay-to-win by definition.

## G §10 — Expansion Categories: Pool vs Rules

Two fundamentally different types of expansion exist and require different design disciplines:

**Type 1 — Card pool expansion:** Adds new cards to existing strategies without changing the rules of the game. More creatures, more spells, more keywords already in the glossary. These are the most common expansions and the safest to design. Balance work is contained to the new cards' relationship with the existing pool.

**Type 2 — Rules/mechanic expansion:** Introduces new card types, new keywords, new phases, or new rule systems. These expansions change what the game is capable of. They are higher-value for engagement (new mechanics create new strategies and a reason to buy the set) and higher-risk for balance (new mechanics interact with the entire existing pool in ways that are hard to predict).

**Design rule:** Every expansion should be mostly Type 1 with at most one Type 2 element per set. Two simultaneous mechanical changes in one expansion double the testing burden and make it difficult to isolate which new mechanic is responsible for any balance problem that emerges after release.

## G §11 — Expansion as a Balance Intervention Tool

A deliberately designed expansion can be the most elegant response to a meta problem. If one faction is significantly under-represented in competitive play, the next expansion can be designed explicitly to give that faction new strategic tools.

**Why this is preferred over patching/banning:** It adds to the game rather than subtracting. Players of the under-performing faction get new cards to be excited about. The balance problem is addressed through design rather than restriction.

**The ethical boundary:** Designing cards that are intentionally weak at launch with the plan to "fix" them in a later expansion is unethical. Players who purchase those cards are being sold intentionally defective product. This has happened in the industry and always damages trust when discovered. The distinction is: designing an expansion to strengthen a weak faction (acceptable) versus designing weak cards on purpose to create a revenue opportunity later (unacceptable).

## G §12 — Release Cadence and the Assembly Line

For a TCG/CCG that has reached commercial viability, expansion release becomes an assembly-line process:
1. New set design and balance testing
2. Art commissioning and production
3. Manufacturing and quality control (physical only)
4. Marketing and community preview
5. Release
6. Start of next set design (should overlap with step 2 of the previous set)

**Cadence factors:** Studio size determines sustainable cadence. A small team cannot safely release four sets per year while maintaining playtesting quality. A large studio (Magic's team size) can sustain multiple releases across multiple formats because different sub-teams handle different formats.

**Announcing the schedule:** Once a schedule is established, announce it and adhere to it. Players, retailers, and tournament organisers plan around set release schedules. An unexpected delay is more damaging than releasing on a slower known cadence. Changes to the schedule must be communicated as early as possible.

**Long-term support: the sequelless game:** Major TCGs and CCGs do not receive sequels. A "sequel" would split the player base and invalidate existing collections. Long-term support through expansions is the only viable model. This means the game's design team must be resourced for indefinite ongoing development, not a fixed project timeline.

## G §13 — Tournaments and Long-Term Ecosystem

For games that reach sufficient scale, an organised play structure becomes part of the product:

**Developer-run championships:** The highest tier of competitive play, funded and organised by the developer or publisher. These events generate community interest, advertisement value, and sponsorship revenue when the game is large enough. They also act as meta showcases — the decks run at championships define the meta for the following season.

**Sponsor-funded side events:** Once the game is established, other companies can fund their own tournaments. The developer defines the rules of sanctioned play; the sponsor provides the venue and prize pool. This expands the tournament ecosystem without direct developer cost.

**Licensing the IP:** Characters and settings from successful TCGs/CCGs are regularly licensed to other game developers. This generates revenue and expands brand awareness without requiring internal development resources. The original game's design team should not be pulled onto licensing projects.

## G §14 — Campaign and Narrative Expansions

Campaign expansions are sets designed for persistent play across multiple sessions. They require mechanics not used in standard competitive formats.

**What makes a campaign expansion different:**
- Cards carry state between sessions (damage, experience, acquired equipment)
- Player choices in one session affect the cards and deck available in the next
- Victory and defeat conditions are different from competitive play — completing a narrative arc rather than winning matches
- Cards may be permanently removed from a campaign deck as part of story consequences

**Card design for campaign:**
- Campaign cards require a persistent state tracking mechanism (sticker, token, separate tracking sheet, or digital companion app)
- Power scaling within a campaign is acceptable — cards that grow stronger over multiple sessions is a campaign design feature, not power creep
- Campaign cards should be clearly marked as campaign-legal only; they are typically unbalanced for competitive play by design

**Campaign expansion release structure:**
- Chapter-based: each set is one chapter of a larger story; decks evolve across chapters
- Standalone campaign: self-contained narrative in one product; no continuation required
- Campaign layer on existing game: campaign rules as an add-on format to a competitive game (the campaign cards are a separate mode, not integrated into the competitive pool)

**Accessibility:** Campaign expansions must provide a catch-up mechanism for new players joining mid-campaign. Either a pre-built starting deck for the current chapter or a rules summary of decisions made in previous chapters.

## G §15 — Design Pillars

Every card game should have three to five design pillars — short statements that define what the game is and is not. Every subsequent design decision is tested against the pillars.

**What a design pillar is:** A constraint that helps designers make decisions consistently. "This game is about meaningful sacrifices" means every card that asks the player to give something up is on-pillar; a card that provides pure benefit with no cost is off-pillar and requires justification.

**Example pillar set:**
1. Every decision should feel meaningful — no free actions, no obvious correct plays
2. Factions feel genuinely different — a player who switches faction is learning a different game
3. The game rewards preparation, not luck — deck construction choices should matter more than draw variance
4. New players can have fun from their first game — the tutorial experience is as important as the competitive ceiling
5. The world comes through in the mechanics — setting and gameplay are inseparable

**How to use pillars:**
- When designing a new card, ask whether it supports or violates each pillar
- When a faction feels wrong, check it against the faction differentiation pillar
- When a new expansion is being planned, check that its theme extends at least one pillar
- When a card is broken, check whether the fix violates a pillar — a balance patch that violates a design pillar is a design failure, not just a balance fix

**Pillar maintenance:** Design pillars should be written at the start of the game's development and revisited at every major expansion. If expansions consistently require exceptions to a pillar, the pillar may need rewriting — or the expansion design may be drifting from the game's identity.
