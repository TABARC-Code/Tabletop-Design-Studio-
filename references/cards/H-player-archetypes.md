# Reference H: Player Archetypes

## H §1 — The Three Core Archetypes

Three fundamental player motivations have been identified across the history of collectible card game design. Every player maps onto one or a blend of two. Designing for all three simultaneously is possible and is what separates long-lived games from short-lived ones.

---

**The Creative (internally: Johnny)**

Motivation: self-expression through deck construction. This player builds decks that nobody else would build. They pursue obscure combos, cards others dismiss, and strategies that work once in ten games but produce a memorable moment when they do.

What this player wants:
- Cards with unusual effects, even if those effects are hard to use
- Enough cards in the pool that truly unique combinations are possible
- A game system where creativity is occasionally rewarded over efficiency
- A sense that their deck is uniquely theirs

What designers must give them:
- Generous card pools with deliberately varied effects
- At least some mechanics that enable combo discovery
- A competitive ceiling — even if their creative decks don't win tournaments, they should sometimes beat "optimal" decks
- Card interactions that weren't explicitly designed but naturally emerge

**Risk:** If the meta becomes too narrow (only one viable deck), the Creative player leaves because there's nothing left to create.

---

**The Power Player (internally: Timmy)**

Motivation: spectacle through overwhelming power. This player wants to play the biggest, most impressive cards on the table and win in the most dramatic fashion possible. Win rate matters less than the quality of the wins when they happen.

What this player wants:
- Big, impressive high-cost cards with dramatic effects
- Winning by board wipe, massive combo, or unstoppable creature is more satisfying than winning efficiently
- Social aspect of play — they want the table to react to their plays
- Collecting impressive cards even if those cards aren't meta-defining

What designers must give them:
- High-cost "bomb" cards with visually and mechanically impressive effects
- At least some viable game plan that involves playing large expensive cards
- A casual play environment where their less-optimal decks are still fun to play
- Foils, alternate art, and cosmetic rarity on impressive-looking cards

**Risk:** If the game becomes too fast and efficient (aggro dominates), the Power Player's big cards never arrive. They stop playing.

---

**The Competitor (internally: Spike)**

Motivation: winning as efficiently as possible. This player min-maxes, studies the meta, and builds decks that maximise win rate. They are the primary tournament player.

What this player wants:
- A fair, well-balanced competitive environment
- Clear meta information about what's currently dominant
- Meaningful strategic decisions that reward preparation and skill
- Formats with structured rules about which cards are legal

What designers must give them:
- A meta that is not dominated by a single deck (undermines the skill dimension)
- Regular meta disruption through expansions and balance changes
- Tournament formats with clear rules and consistent enforcement
- Incentive for top tournament performance (not just glory; product prizes, status)

**Risk:** If the meta is too solved (one deck is always best), the Competitor leaves because there's nothing left to optimise. If balance is poor, they lose to card quality rather than play quality, which feels unfair.

## H §2 — Deck Strategy Taxonomy

These six strategic archetypes appear in every card game that has been played at scale. Design should ensure all six are viable in the base game.

**Aggro (All-out attack):** Win fast. Play low-cost high-pressure cards. Never let the opponent stabilise. These decks want to end games before turn 6. Primary audience: Competitor.

**Control (Defence and denial):** Prevent the opponent from doing anything meaningful. Then win slowly through inevitable attrition. These decks say "no" to everything. Primary audience: Competitor; secondary: Creative.

**Midrange (Balance):** Neither fastest nor most reactive. Plays efficiently at each cost bracket, adapting to the opponent. The most forgiving deck type for new players. Primary audience: all types.

**Combo (Big combination):** Build a specific combination of cards over multiple turns, then execute for a disproportionate effect. When it works, it's spectacular. When it doesn't, it crashes. Primary audience: Creative and Power Player.

**Cheese:** Exploit a specific card interaction in a way that was unintended by design, often using a low-cost card in combination with other cards that amplify it far beyond its stated effect. Sometimes becomes a legitimate meta strategy if it's consistent. Primary audience: Competitor (pragmatically) and Creative (experimentally).

**Turtle (Stall):** Delay everything for as long as possible while building toward an overwhelming late-game position. Frustrating to play against; rewarding to execute. Primary audience: Creative and Competitor.

**Design requirement:** For a healthy game, at least four of these six should be viable in the competitive meta at any time. When fewer than four are viable, the meta is solved and player diversity collapses.

## H §3 — Designing for Audience Fit

When designing a new card game, identify the primary audience:

**Casual-first design:**
- Lower deck construction complexity
- Lower minimum viable card count for a functional deck
- Starter decks that are genuinely competitive, not just tutorial shells
- Fewer mandatory rares to compete
- Primary archetypes: Midrange, Aggro (easy to understand), Power Player support
- Suitable for: family audiences, game shop introductory products, IP-based card games

**Competitive-first design:**
- Deep deck-building meta from launch
- Designed around tournament formats from day one
- Power Player and Creative archetypes well-served from base set
- Meaningful decision density per turn
- Suitable for: TCG market, collector communities, esports-adjacent audiences

**Both simultaneously:** This is what Magic, Pokémon, and Yu-Gi-Oh achieve over decades. It requires:
- Different format structures for different audiences (kitchen table vs Standard vs Legacy)
- Starter products for new players that don't require engagement with the competitive meta
- Communication that acknowledges both audiences

## H §4 — Player Archetype and Faction Design

Factions should appeal differently to different archetypes:

- The aggressive faction (high-attack low-cost cards) attracts Competitors running aggro and Power Players who like to smash
- The combo faction (cards with complex keyword interactions) attracts Creative players
- The control faction (reactive, denial-based mechanics) attracts Competitors running control
- The narrative faction (flavour-heavy, lore-deep, world-connected cards) attracts Creative and Power Players who collect for aesthetics

When designing a faction, ask: which archetype does this faction primarily serve? Then ensure it does that job clearly. A faction that tries to serve all archetypes equally often serves none distinctively.

## H §5 — The Collector/Aesthete Archetype

A fourth player type operates largely outside the competitive game: the Collector. This player's primary motivation is ownership and display, not play.

**What the Collector wants:**
- Rare, visually impressive physical cards as objects
- Complete sets — owning every card from a given set or era
- Limited edition and alternate art versions
- Card condition matters: near-mint or graded copies preferred over played ones
- The secondary market ecosystem (buying, selling, trading)

**What the Collector does not need from game design:**
- Competitive balance (they may never play the cards)
- Meta viability (a broken or banned card may be more valuable to them)
- New mechanics (iconic cards from formative eras are more desirable than mechanically novel new ones)

**Design implications:**
- Collectors create demand for premium product tiers (collector boosters, alternate art series, limited print runs)
- Collectors sustain the secondhand market and card pricing ecosystem
- Reprint decisions affect Collectors disproportionately — reprinting a rare card reduces its value and can damage collector trust
- Collectors are less sensitive to game balance changes and more sensitive to supply decisions (print runs, reprints, exclusive art)

**The Aesthete variant:** Similar to the Collector but motivated by art and lore rather than rarity and value. Aesthetes buy cards for the illustration, the flavour text, the world-building. They are the primary audience for flavour-first card design. They may play the game but their purchasing decisions are driven by whether a new set's art direction resonates, not whether its cards are meta-relevant.

## H §6 — New Player Design Requirements

New players are not a player archetype in the motivational sense — they are in a transitional state before settling into one or more archetypes. But they have specific design requirements that are different from any established player:

**What new players need:**
- A tutorial that teaches the win condition within 2 minutes (see Q §11)
- Starter decks that are functional and teach the game's core strategy, not just placeholders
- Rules language simple enough to understand without external resources
- A first session experience that produces at least one memorable moment (a dramatic play, a close game, a surprising interaction)
- A clear path to improving — next step purchases, beginner-friendly formats

**What breaks new player retention:**
- First session losses that feel determined by card quality, not decisions (pay-to-win perception)
- Rules disputes in the first session (rules writing failure)
- No casual mode — being forced into competitive play before ready
- Starter decks that lose to any other deck without a chance

**The conversion funnel:** New player → casual player (archetype unknown) → committed player (archetype emerging) → invested player (archetype established). Design must support this journey, not just the endpoints.

## H §7 — Player Retention and Lapsed Player Reactivation

**Retention curves:** Player engagement in TCGs/CCGs typically follows this pattern: high engagement at launch/onboarding → natural decline as novelty wears off → retention hook (tournament involvement, collector investment, community relationships) → either stabilisation at a committed level or lapse.

**What causes lapse:**
- Meta stagnation (Spike leaves when there's nothing to optimise)
- Collection ceiling (Collector leaves when they feel complete)
- Social context loss (player whose group stopped playing loses the reason to play)
- Barrier to re-entry (returning player faces a large card pool they don't know and feel behind)

**Lapsed player reactivation:**
- Welcome-back products (a starter bundle for returning players that catches them up without requiring them to buy everything they missed)
- Explicit acknowledgment that the game has changed (changelog or "what's new" content aimed at returning players)
- Entry-point formats (formats that use a smaller subset of cards so returning players aren't immediately overwhelmed by the full pool)
- Nostalgia products (reprints of iconic cards from the era when the player was active)

**Design principle:** Lapsed players are not the same as new players. They know the game but feel disconnected from it. Reactivation requires reducing the re-entry barrier, not re-teaching the basics.
