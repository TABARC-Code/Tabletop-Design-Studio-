# Reference I: Theme and World

## I §1 — Theme Defined

Theme is not art direction. Theme is the meaningful relationship between what the game is about (setting, story, character) and how the game is played (mechanics, decisions, outcomes).

A card game with good theme makes the player feel like their strategic decisions are expressions of the fictional world. A card game with bad theme makes the player feel like they're doing arithmetic with pictures of orcs.

**The key question:** When a player makes a strategic decision in your game, does that decision feel like it belongs in the world you've described?

## I §2 — Knitted vs Layered Theme

Two fundamental modes of thematic integration:

**Knitted theme:** Mechanics and setting developed simultaneously. The game's mechanical logic emerges from the setting logic. Theme and mechanics are inseparable — rethming the game would require redesigning some mechanics.

Characteristics:
- Faction mechanics feel like expressions of faction identity, not arbitrary stat differences
- Card names and effects are legible from the world-building alone
- New players grasp the game's identity quickly because the fiction explains the mechanics
- Examples: Gwent (political factions with distinct tactical philosophies expressed through play), the elemental colour wheel in Magic

**Layered theme:** Mechanics designed first; setting applied on top. The mechanics work regardless of setting. Theme is an overlay.

Characteristics:
- Mechanics could theoretically support a completely different setting
- Strong mechanical design; sometimes weak setting coherence
- Easier to retheme if the setting stops resonating
- Risk: the game feels "painted" — setting doesn't justify mechanics, just decorates them

**Which to choose:** Knitted theme creates stronger player identity and loyalty. It takes longer to design correctly. If you have a setting you believe in, start there and let the mechanics emerge from it. If you have a mechanic you believe in, design it well first and then find the setting that best expresses it.

**Partially knitted (common middle ground):** Most successful TCGs have tightly knitted faction/setting identity (the faction's culture explains why they play the way they do) but loosely layered aesthetic elements (the art and flavour text could be swapped without affecting mechanics). This is the pragmatic target.

## I §3 — Faction Identity as Thematic Expression

Every faction's mechanical identity should be traceable to its thematic identity.

**Design exercise:** For each faction, complete this sentence:
*"This faction plays [strategic style] because in the fiction, they are [setting identity]."*

Examples:
- "This faction plays control/denial because they are a secretive intelligence network that wins through information control and sabotage."
- "This faction plays aggro because they are a warrior culture where honour demands direct confrontation."
- "This faction plays combo/synergy because they are a scholarly order that studies how different magical elements interact."

If you cannot complete this sentence for a faction, the faction's mechanics and setting are not knitted. This is a design problem to solve before manufacturing.

## I §4 — Keyword Names as World-Building

Keyword names are a surface where theme lands most frequently. Players say keyword names constantly during play. They should feel like they belong to the world.

**Functional but world-generic keywords (acceptable):** Flying, Charge, First Strike, Regeneration, Taunt. Players understand these immediately but they don't carry setting specificity.

**World-knitted keywords (preferred):** Keywords named after in-world concepts. "Blood Tithe" as a mechanic for sacrificing creatures to gain power feels like it belongs to a necromantic faction. "Wildgrowth" as a heal-and-protect keyword feels like a forest faction's identity. "Iron Protocol" as a keyword that activates when specific combat conditions are met feels mechanical-faction appropriate.

**Naming discipline:** Keyword names should be two to three words maximum. They must be pronounceable and memorable. They should not accidentally share conceptual space with another keyword in the same game.

## I §5 — Flavour Text

Flavour text is the narrative content in the card's text box, typically in italics, separate from rules text.

**Functions:**
- Advances the game's ongoing narrative
- Gives character voice to cards, making collections feel like character rosters not stat sheets
- Provides lore access points for players interested in the setting
- Rewards collectors who read every card

**Rules for flavour text:**
- Flavour text never modifies or overrides rules text (if it seems to, the rules text is ambiguous)
- Flavour text should be short: one to three sentences
- Flavour text can be attributed to a character (adding to the fiction) or unattributed (setting description)
- Avoid flavour text that makes light of serious violence or death in ways inconsistent with the game's tone
- High-rarity cards should have flavour text that rewards the player's investment in acquiring them

## I §6 — Thematic Actions and Mechanical Resonance

The most satisfying card games are those where performing the mechanical action feels like performing the thematic action.

**Thematic action examples:**
- Sacrificing a lower-level creature to summon a powerful undead lord (mechanics reinforce the necromantic theme)
- Playing a combination of fire and air cards to summon a lightning storm creature (element combination has natural logic)
- Drawing multiple cards by playing a scholar-faction ability called "Research" (the action matches the faction's intellectual identity)

**Testing thematic resonance:** After designing a card, read its name, look at its art, then read its effect. Does the effect feel like what that name and art suggest it would do? If there's a mismatch, either change the name and art or change the effect.

## I §7 — World-Building Layers for CCG/TCG

A complete setting for a card game requires these layers:

1. **Cosmological frame:** What is the world? What forces shape it? (magic, science, divine power, political)
2. **Faction origins:** Why does each faction exist? What is the root of their conflict?
3. **Historical context:** What brought the game's world to this point? (useful for narrative expansions)
4. **Mechanical expression:** Which setting elements become game mechanics?
5. **Visual language:** What does each faction look like? What colours, symbols, aesthetics define them?
6. **Ongoing narrative:** How does the world change across expansions? Are there events players can follow?

Card games that have long-term player engagement maintain an ongoing narrative that expansions advance. Players who care about the story are more likely to buy sets for narrative completion, not just card power.

## I §8 — Retheme Doctrine

A retheme replaces the setting while preserving the mechanics. See K §1 for refit context.

**When to retheme:**
- The original setting no longer resonates with the target audience
- A licensing or IP change requires setting replacement
- The mechanics are strong but the setting has caused accessibility problems

**What must be preserved in a retheme:**
- All keyword mechanics (these are the game's identity at the rules level)
- All card type categories
- All resource system rules
- All win condition structures

**What changes in a retheme:**
- All card names and flavour text
- All art direction
- All faction names and identities (can keep faction archetypes; change their thematic expression)
- Keyword names can be renamed if they were too setting-specific

**Retheme warning:** A retheme that requires changing mechanics is not a retheme, it is a new game. Be honest about which one you are doing.

## I §9 — IP Adaptation: Designing in an Existing Property

Card games set in existing intellectual properties (established fictional universes, licensed brands, films, game franchises) face a different design challenge from original settings: the world already exists and the designer must work within it rather than building from scratch.

**IP adaptation challenges:**
- Character power hierarchy is already defined in the source material — a card game that makes an iconic powerful character weak feels wrong, but making them mechanically unbeatable creates balance problems
- Faction structure may not match card game faction design needs (a universe with 20 named factions requires reducing to 4–6 playable ones)
- Fan expectations are high and specific — community members who know the IP deeply will notice inaccuracies in flavour text, card abilities that contradict established lore, and character interactions that don't match the source

**IP adaptation process:**
1. Identify the core dramatic tension of the IP (good vs evil, faction war, survival) — this becomes the win condition framing
2. Map IP factions or character groups onto the required number of card game factions (consolidate minor groups; split oversized factions if their internal conflict is interesting)
3. Identify the IP's signature abilities, powers, and mechanics — these become keyword candidates
4. Define the IP's "power ranking" for characters — translate this into rarity tiers, not absolute power
5. Establish the canonical time period the game is set in — this determines which characters exist and in what state

**IP canon management:** Expansions must maintain internal consistency with the game's defined canon period. If a new expansion set in a later story period contradicts the earlier set, players will notice. Maintain a canon document that all expansion designers reference.

## I §10 — Tone Management

Card game settings occupy a tone spectrum from comedic to grimdark. Getting tone wrong for the target audience is as damaging as getting the mechanics wrong.

**Tone indicators:**
- Art style (cartoon/stylised vs realistic/detailed)
- Flavour text register (humorous vs serious vs poetic vs matter-of-fact)
- What the cards depict (consequence-free combat vs death with weight)
- How loss is framed (it's a game vs you've failed to protect something important)

**Tone consistency:** Every card must read as belonging to the same game. A single out-of-tone card (a comedic card in a serious game, or a grimdark card in a family game) destroys the world's coherence. Establish the tone document before art direction begins.

**Tone and audience:** Family-friendly games (Pokémon) use stylised art, consequence-free conflict, and adventurous rather than violent framing. Mature games can depict death, moral ambiguity, and darker themes. Both can have excellent game design. The mistake is mixing them: a game that starts with family-friendly framing then introduces grimdark cards loses both audiences.

## I §11 — Lore Delivery Beyond Cards

The card game's setting can be delivered through multiple channels beyond flavour text:

**Rulebook fiction:** An in-world story framing the rules. The most famous example is using narrative to introduce mechanics (a story about a duel that demonstrates the rules). This approach requires excellent writing — bad rulebook fiction reads as padding and is skipped entirely.

**Expansion storybooks:** Short fiction booklets included in expansion packaging, advancing the world's narrative. Rewards collectors and lore-focused players. Does not require engagement from competitive players.

**Online lore content:** Card-by-card lore entries on the official website, searchable by faction and character. Community wikis typically build this organically if the setting is rich enough.

**Designer commentary:** Expansion previews with the design team explaining each new card's place in the story. Builds community investment in the design process.

**Design rule:** Lore delivery channels must be consistent with each other. A character's backstory on the website cannot contradict their flavour text on the card. Assign one person as lore continuity editor before any lore content is published.
