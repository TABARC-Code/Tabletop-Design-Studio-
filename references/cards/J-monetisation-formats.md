# Reference J: Monetisation and Formats

## J §1 — TCG vs CCG vs LCG Economics

**TCG (Trading Card Game):** Physical cards in booster packs with randomised rare distribution. Players buy packs and trade to acquire specific cards. Revenue model: pack sales, repeat purchases for new sets, secondary market (does not directly benefit publisher but benefits ecosystem).

**CCG (Collectible Card Game):** Digital format. Random pack opening usually replaced by dust/wildcard systems (craft specific cards after enough duplicates). Revenue model: direct pack purchases, cosmetic sales, battle pass, premium season content.

**LCG (Living Card Game):** Fixed distribution — each expansion contains exactly the listed cards with no randomisation. Players know exactly what they're buying. Revenue model: expansion sales. Lower barrier to entry; lower repeat purchase pressure; smaller total revenue ceiling.

**Choosing a model:**
- TCG: highest revenue potential; highest player frustration potential (bad pulls, pay-to-win risk)
- CCG: good revenue with cosmetics; needs large player base to sustain
- LCG: most player-friendly; requires careful expansion pricing to remain commercially viable
- Hybrid (TCG with LCG starter decks): common compromise; random packs for competitive content, fixed decks for new players

## J §2 — Booster Pack Design

Booster packs are the primary TCG revenue unit. Their structure directly affects player satisfaction.

**Pack composition guidelines:**
- Define the guaranteed rarity minimum per pack (e.g. 1 rare per 15-card pack; 1 guaranteed foil per 36-pack booster box)
- Guarantee at least one card that any player would be glad to get (not all filler commons)
- Publish the pull rates for each rarity tier; transparency builds trust
- Pity system for digital packs: after N packs without a legendary, guarantee one (industry standard now expected)

**Booster set size:** Large sets (250+ cards) give packs more variety but dilute specific card acquisition. Small sets (100–150 cards) make each pack feel more targeted. Most successful physical sets are 150–270 cards.

**Draft packs:** Smaller (8–15 cards per pack), designed to provide a balanced draft experience. Every draft pack should contain cards that enable playable deck archetypes. Draft formats also serve as product entry points for new players.

## J §3 — Secondhand Market

Physical TCGs generate a secondhand market that the publisher does not control. This creates:

**Positive effects:**
- Players can acquire specific cards without buying random packs
- Rare cards gain monetary value, increasing the perceived value of collecting
- Casual players can sell unneeded cards to fund desired ones

**Negative effects:**
- High-demand rares can be priced out of reach for competitive play (pay-to-win adjacency)
- Publisher loses revenue to secondary market resellers
- Card prices can spike immediately after a card becomes meta-defining, before a reprint can occur

**Publisher management options:**
- Reprint high-demand cards in accessibly priced products (reduces secondary prices; some collector backlash)
- Create alternative "functional reprint" cards with different names/art but equivalent effects (preserves collector value of originals)
- Announce reprint policy clearly: players and retailers need to know whether reprints will occur

## J §4 — Format Design

Formats define which cards are legal in competitive play. Format design is the primary tool for managing the competitive health of a game over years.

**Standard (Rotating):** Only cards from the most recent N sets are legal. Older sets rotate out on a schedule. This keeps the card pool manageable, keeps new players competitive, and ensures that power creep in older sets eventually becomes irrelevant. Most competitive TCG/CCGs use this as their primary format.

**Legacy/Extended (Non-rotating):** All legally printed cards are legal. Highest card power ceiling; highest deck cost; attracts veteran players; requires a ban/restricted list to prevent degenerate combos from the full card history.

**Draft:** Players open packs during an event and build decks from what they open. Levels the financial playing field; skill-tests deck-building; provides an entry point for new players. Requires sets designed with draft in mind (cards that work together at lower power curves than constructed).

**Sealed:** Players receive a fixed set of sealed packs and build a deck from only those cards. Similar to draft but without the pick rotation. More luck-dependent than draft; quicker to execute.

**Commander/Multiplayer format:** 3–4 player games with modified deck rules (100-card singleton decks, etc.). Creates a different play experience; very popular in casual communities. Requires separate balance consideration — cards designed for 1v1 play may be too weak or too powerful in multiplayer.

**Format rotation schedule:** Define when sets rotate out of Standard before the first set launches. Players and retailers plan around this schedule. Changing it mid-cycle damages trust.

## J §5 — Digital Monetisation

Digital CCGs have more flexibility in monetisation but also more expectation of cosmetic-primary models.

**Acceptable digital monetisation:**
- Cosmetic card variants (animated, special art, holiday themes) — no gameplay effect
- Cosmetic game board and background purchases
- Battle pass with primarily cosmetic rewards and earned currency
- Expansion packs with guaranteed card acquisition paths (wildcards, dust systems)
- Solo adventure content as paid product

**Problematic digital monetisation:**
- Gameplay-affecting cards locked exclusively behind premium purchase (pay-to-win)
- No free path to acquire competitive cards for new players
- Loot boxes with no pity system and no published pull rates
- Limited-time exclusive gameplay cards (creates fear-of-missing-out pressure)

**Free-to-play entry:** Digital CCGs should provide a viable free-to-play path for at least casual competitive play. Players who start free and enjoy the game become paying customers; players who hit a hard paywall before enjoying the game leave.

## J §6 — Starter Deck Design

Starter decks are the primary new player acquisition tool. They must be:

- **Genuinely playable:** A starter deck that loses every game against casual decks will not convert new players
- **Teachable:** The deck should demonstrate the game's core mechanics without overwhelming with special cases
- **A foundation, not a ceiling:** Starters should be improvable through normal card acquisition — not so complete that the player has nowhere to go, not so incomplete that they immediately need 30 more cards to compete
- **Faction-representative:** Each starter deck should introduce one or two factions clearly, not sample all factions superficially

**Starter deck common mistakes:**
- Filling starters with bulk common cards that no competitive player uses (players notice)
- Making starters so different from competitive decks that they feel like a different game
- Not including a quick-reference card with the turn structure

## J §7 — Premium Currency: Dual Acquisition Model

CCGs that use premium currency should provide two acquisition paths: purchase with real money AND earn through play. The earn-through-play path is not charity — it is a player retention mechanism that also converts players into paying customers.

**Earn-through-play structures:**
- Daily challenges: specific in-game actions that award small amounts of premium currency or booster packs on completion
- Seasonal challenges: longer-horizon objectives with larger rewards
- Ranked play rewards: currency for hitting rank thresholds each season
- Tournament entry fee packs: tournaments cost premium currency to enter but pay out packs and currency to top finishers

**Why the free path matters:** Players who cannot compete because they cannot afford cards leave. Players who earn cards through play and feel progression momentum become paying customers when they want specific cards faster. The free path must be viable enough to build a competitive casual deck over time — not competitive at the highest level, but not frustratingly slow either.

## J §8 — Limited Print Runs and Exclusive Product

TCGs create limited-run booster boxes and special products for collector and event purposes. Design rules for these products:

**Guaranteed rarity:** Limited-run boxes that guarantee a higher-rarity card or a specific card are a commercial tool that also manages the secondhand market. Telling buyers they will receive at least one card from a specific tier reduces the random frustration of the standard booster.

**Rate display requirement:** Even for limited-run products, pull rates for every rarity tier must be displayed. No exception based on product type or exclusivity. This is a legal requirement in many territories and a trust requirement everywhere.

**Exclusive art as collector product:** Limited-run products can include art-exclusive versions of existing cards (same game text, different illustration or foil treatment). These satisfy the collector drive without creating gameplay imbalance. Never make a limited-run product the only source of a card with unique gameplay effects.

**Collector tins:** Pre-assembled products with multiple packs from different sets plus promotional content. Allow players who missed older sets to access older cards. Useful for new player acquisition and for fans who want to collect iconic sets.

## J §9 — The Secondhand Market: Economics and Management

The physical TCG secondhand market is not controlled by the publisher beyond the original sale (first-sale doctrine: the publisher's revenue claim ends at first retail purchase). However, the publisher's design decisions shape the secondhand market significantly.

**Card grading:** Third-party companies (PSA, Beckett) grade the physical condition of cards on a numeric scale. High-grade rare cards from early print runs sell for multiples of their ungraded value. Graded cards are typically sealed in display cases and cannot be played. This is a collector economy running parallel to the player economy.

**Print run decisions:** Lower print runs create scarcer cards, increasing secondhand market prices. Higher print runs democratise access. The tension: scarce expensive cards drive collector interest and secondary market activity (which generates buzz for the game); accessible cards keep competitive play fair. Most games err toward accessibility for cards with gameplay significance and allow scarcity to operate for cosmetic and collector products.

**What the designer can and cannot control:**
- Can control: print run sizes, reprint decisions, reserve list policies (promise not to reprint certain cards)
- Cannot control: secondhand pricing, condition standards, speculation
- Can influence: secondhand market health by making competitive cards accessible through reprint products

**First-sale doctrine implication:** A thriving secondhand market creates community and cultural relevance for the game without generating direct publisher revenue. Designers should be aware that banning a card makes that card more valuable in the secondhand market, not less — collector demand for banned cards is historically high.

## J §10 — Format Design: Four Baseline Formats

Any TCG/CCG large enough to have multiple expansions and a banned list needs at minimum four formats. All four should be available at launch or within the first major update:

**Standard/ranked play:** Normal competitive play with the current active card sets and the official ban list enforced. The competitive health of this format is the primary responsibility of the balance team.

**Free/open play:** Any cards legal, ban list not enforced. For casual play between friends or experimental deck testing. The format where banned cards still get used.

**Tournament play:** Structured competitive play at events, with additional restrictions on which sets are legal. May have a smaller legal set list than Standard to focus the meta for competition.

**Sealed-deck tournament:** Players receive sealed booster packs at the event and build a deck from what they open. Levels the financial playing field — the cards available are determined by luck of the packs, not by collection depth. Tests limited-format deck-building skill.

**Additional formats as the game grows:**
- Commander/multiplayer: modified deck rules for 3–4 player games
- Speed/entry format: simplified rules for new players, with cross-compatible cards
- Puzzle mode: fixed board state, player must win in X turns — teaches advanced play
- Fan-created formats: if a fan format achieves significant organic adoption, consider official recognition with a supported booster product

**Format legality in the deck editor:** Every digital CCG must include legality filters in the deck builder. A player should be able to see immediately whether a card is legal in Standard, whether it is on the ban list, and which formats it can be used in. Failure to implement this creates player friction that reduces competitive participation.

## J §11 — Side Deck and Tournament Deck Management

Tournament formats may include a side deck: a small pool of additional cards (typically 10–15) that players can swap into their main deck between rounds in a match.

**Purpose:** Allows players to adapt their strategy between games in the same match based on what they learned about the opponent's deck. Creates a second layer of strategic decision-making (not just "what is the best deck" but "what adjustments do I make against this opponent").

**Design implications:** Cards that are too narrow for the main deck but highly effective against specific strategies become side deck staples. This legitimises designing narrowly targeted counter-cards that would be too situational for main deck inclusion.

**Format variant — multiple tournament decks:** Some formats allow players to bring multiple decks and must switch deck between rounds. Higher complexity; used in formats designed for experienced players only.
