# Reference K: Refit and Modernisation

## K §1 — Refit Assessment

A refit is the redesign of an existing card game to bring it into a modern context. This covers:
- Updating rules language for clarity and consistency
- Rebalancing an older card pool that has become lopsided
- Modernising the monetisation or format structure
- Refitting the game for a new edition or publisher

Before deciding how to refit, answer these diagnostic questions:

**What still works?**
- Which mechanics have remained fun and functional?
- Which cards are still talked about positively?
- What is the game's core identity that players would mourn if lost?
- What format or play mode has survived the longest?

**What is broken?**
- Which cards or combos have made the game unplayable in their presence?
- Which rules are consistently misunderstood or misapplied?
- Which mechanics feel outdated compared to current card game design standards?
- Which factions or deck archetypes have no viable strategy?

**What is the refit target?**
- Restoring competitive viability of an older game (balance-focused refit)
- Bringing a physical game to digital (platform refit)
- Re-theming for a new setting or license (thematic refit — see I §8)
- Full edition reset with redesigned rules and cards (major refit)

## K §2 — Legacy Card Management

The hardest problem in any refit is managing the existing card pool's relationship with new or changed rules.

**Option 1: Format quarantine**
Divide cards into legal formats. Legacy format = all cards legal with appropriate ban list. Standard format = only new/refitted cards legal. Players who have old cards still have a place to use them.

**Option 2: Errata**
Officially change the rules text on existing cards without physically reprinting them. Errata is published in the official FAQ and takes precedence over the printed card text.

*When to errata:* A card is consistently misplayed due to ambiguous text; a keyword has been redefined; a card creates an unintended degenerate interaction with a new rule.

*When not to errata:* To change a card's power level retroactively. Power errata (changing a card's stats or effects to nerf it) should be the absolute last resort after all other interventions have failed. Players purchased the card with specific expectations.

**Option 3: Rotation**
Old sets are no longer legal in the primary competitive format. Cards are not gone — they remain legal in Legacy formats — but their presence in the active meta ends. This is the cleanest long-term solution to power creep from old card pools.

**Option 4: Emergency ban**
A specific card is removed from all competitive formats immediately due to a critical balance or rules problem. Should be accompanied by a public statement explaining the decision. In a physical TCG, this may include a refund/exchange program for affected cards.

## K §3 — Rules Language Modernisation

Older card games often have rules language that was clear when written but has become ambiguous through card interactions that were never anticipated.

**Modernisation priorities:**
1. Establish a consistent terminology glossary — every mechanical term used on any card must be defined in one place
2. Audit every card that has been ruled on in official errata/FAQ and rewrite its text to make the ruling unnecessary
3. Identify and resolve all "this card does X but under condition Y it is unclear" interactions
4. Rewrite rules using active-voice, specific-noun construction: "When this card is destroyed" not "Upon destruction"
5. Eliminate pronoun ambiguity: "it" is insufficient when there are multiple "its" in the same effect

**The "Say Why" principle applied to refit:** Every rule that survives the refit should have a documented reason for surviving. If you cannot articulate why a rule exists, it may not need to exist.

## K §4 — Keyword Modernisation

Legacy keyword systems often have one or more of these problems:
- Too many keywords with similar effects (consolidated into one keyword in modern games)
- Keywords with names that no longer reflect their function (setting changed; keyword name didn't)
- Keywords with undefined interaction with newer mechanics
- Keywords that exist in the text of only one or two cards (not worth being a keyword; should be written out as card text)

**Keyword modernisation process:**
1. Catalogue every keyword in the existing card pool
2. Group keywords by function: all damage-on-entry effects, all protection effects, all draw effects, etc.
3. Identify groups where multiple keywords do the same thing — consolidate into one with the clearest name
4. Identify keywords used on fewer than three cards — demote to card-specific text
5. Identify keywords with no defined counter or interaction with other keywords — write the interaction rule
6. Test the modernised keyword set against the complete refitted card pool

## K §5 — Refit Communication

A refit changes things players are invested in. Communication is as important as design.

**What to communicate before a refit launches:**
- Which cards will be changed and why
- Which formats will remain available for players with old cards
- Whether there will be any compensation for significantly changed or banned cards
- The timeline for changes to take effect

**Community management during a refit:**
- Acknowledge what players loved about the original game
- Distinguish between changes made for balance (necessary) and changes made for thematic or commercial reasons (explain them separately)
- Create a test period where the refitted rules are trialled before becoming official
- Invite player feedback on specific changes before finalising — players often identify problems designers miss

**What to avoid:**
- Announcing a refit without explaining what was wrong with the original
- Changing multiple fundamental systems simultaneously without a transition period
- Removing beloved elements without acknowledgment ("just removing X because Y" without respecting the community's attachment)

## K §6 — Ban List Revision and Rotation Management

A ban list is a living document, not a permanent judgment. Doctrine for maintaining it:

**Rotating ban list:** Some games refresh the ban list on a seasonal schedule, letting previously banned cards return and newly problematic cards enter. This keeps the meta in flux — no one strategy can dominate permanently because the legal card pool changes with each season. The rotation must be announced well in advance for tournament players to adapt.

**Individual card vs whole set banning:** Banning individual cards removes specific problems. Banning entire sets (for tournament play) cleans up large swathes of older content at once, usually used to enforce the rotation of a Standard format. Both approaches are valid; the right choice depends on whether the problem is one specific card or an entire era of design that no longer fits the game's current power level.

**Minimum pool before rotation:** Set rotation for Standard formats only makes sense when the remaining legal card pool is large enough to support the variety of strategies the game requires. Rotating out sets when fewer than three seasons of cards remain in the pool can make certain strategies unplayable, which is as damaging as having a dominant strategy. The practical rule: do not rotate a set out of Standard unless there are at least two full seasons of cards remaining in the legal pool.

**Reintroducing a banned card:** The primary question is whether the card that enabled the broken strategy in the original context still enables it now. As the pool grows, a card that was broken in combination with cards from its era may no longer have the same support network. Test it: run the original broken strategy with the card restored and the current pool. If the old combo no longer assembles or no longer dominates, the card can return. If a newer card has since been printed that provides the same function more safely, there is no reason to reintroduce the older one. Permanently banned cards are those where any context that makes them legal also makes them dominant.
