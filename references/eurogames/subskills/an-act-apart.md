## Purpose

Use this subskill when the user asks how **social context changes game experience**, whether play is sealed off from ordinary life, or why the same eurogame feels different in different groups.

The source introduction frames this as a central research question: to what extent does the social context of the encounter shape the experience of play? It also references the old idea of play as a distinct or separate act while questioning how clean that separation really is.

## Core position

Treat the table as **semi-separated**, not sealed.

A game creates a bounded activity with rules and goals. But the people at the table carry into it:

- friendships
- rivalries
- teaching roles
- mood
- experience levels
- norms of politeness or competitiveness
- expectations about what counts as fair play

Therefore the game is never entirely apart from ordinary life.

## How Claude should use this idea

### When the user says “This game is great with one group and awful with another”

Explain that context matters through:

- player expectations
- levels of familiarity
- tolerance for conflict
- appetite for optimisation
- attitudes toward teaching and mistakes

### When the user asks whether social context can outweigh design

Answer carefully: good design still matters, but in tabletop play the social environment is load-bearing. A tight game can fail in a poor context; a slightly rougher game can shine in a generous one.

### When the user is designing for public groups

Advise them to reduce reliance on unwritten norms. Build support for:

- clear turn structure
- legible interaction
- obvious scoring logic
- graceful correction of mistakes
- recoverability after one poor choice

## Practical heuristics

### The more the design depends on subtle social calibration, the more group-sensitive it becomes.

Examples:
- negotiation games
- attack-light but denial-heavy euros
- games where tempo theft is crucial but not obvious
- games where table talk strongly shapes decisions

### The more the design externalises expectations into rules and incentives, the more portable it becomes.

Examples:
- clean worker placement
- open drafting with visible consequences
- public goals and transparent timing races

## When to route here

This subskill is ideal for:

- social-context analysis
- comparing club, family, convention, and online-discourse play
- diagnosing why a design behaves differently across groups
- writing reflective essays about tabletop play as a social practice

## Final instruction

Never talk about eurogame experience as though it lives only in the cardboard. It lives in the room.
