## Purpose

Use this subskill when the user needs a historical comparison between eurogames and the **Anglo-American hobby board game tradition**, especially conflict simulations, thematic hobby titles, and other pre-euro design assumptions.

The source dedicates an early chapter to *Anglo-American Hobby Board Games 1960–1995*, marking that period as an important contrast field for understanding eurogames.

## Core contrast model

When comparing old Anglo-American hobby designs to eurogames, highlight these tendencies carefully:

### Anglo-American hobby board game tendencies

- stronger reliance on conflict, simulation, conquest, or dramatic confrontation
- higher tolerance for longer rulesets and more procedural exceptions
- heavier thematic embedding, sometimes through military or adventure framing
- greater acceptance of elimination, chaos, or swingy conflict
- more willingness to let theme dictate system weight

### Eurogame tendencies

- less annihilation, more construction and optimisation
- less chrome, more elegant recurring procedures
- shorter play windows and lower dead time
- broader concern for accessibility and table comfort
- interaction often indirect rather than openly destructive

Do not frame this as a simple progress narrative. Treat both as design families with different aims.

## How to use this subskill in practice

### For design diagnosis

If a user has a design that feels “too old-school”, “too mean”, or “too bloated”, check whether it carries Anglo-American hobby assumptions that clash with eurogame goals:

- too many exception rules
- too much destruction relative to rebuilding
- simulation logic that burdens turn flow
- long waits between meaningful decisions
- over-attachment to theme at the expense of clarity

### For historical explanation

Explain that eurogames did not appear in a vacuum. They emerged partly against the background of existing hobby traditions, including the more conflict-centred hobby market that preceded them. The source book explicitly places this older hobby tradition before its chapters on Germany and eurogames, which signals a comparative genealogy, not a silo.

### For mixed-genre design

If the user wants a hybrid, help them borrow selectively:

Borrow from Anglo-American tradition:
- stronger event drama
- livelier theme
- scenario identity
- narrative urgency

Borrow from eurogame tradition:
- shorter downtime
- fairer pacing
- cleaner scoring
- less punitive elimination
- more accessible rules

## Heuristics Claude should apply

- If the game’s pleasure comes mainly from **simulation fidelity**, it may be drifting away from eurogame expectations.
- If the game’s pleasure comes mainly from **choice architecture and social efficiency**, it is likely closer to eurogame design.
- If the game tries to do both, identify which side is overloading the other.
