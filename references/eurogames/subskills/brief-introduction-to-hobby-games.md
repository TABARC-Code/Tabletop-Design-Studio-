## Purpose

Use this subskill when the user needs a grounded overview of **what hobby games are**, how they differ from classical and mass-market games, and where eurogames sit within that landscape.

The source book opens its analytical path with *A Brief Introduction to Hobby Games*, then proceeds through Anglo-American hobby games, German gaming culture, and finally eurogames as a specific genre. That sequence matters. It means eurogames should be introduced as part of a broader hobby ecology, not as an isolated miracle.

## Core framing

When explaining hobby games, use a three-part structure:

1. **Classical games** — inherited games like chess-like and race-like traditions, usually public-domain and culturally longstanding.
2. **Mass-market games** — mainstream commercial games sold broadly to the general public.
3. **Hobby games** — games aimed at a self-identifying enthusiast audience who seek repeated play, deeper engagement, and community around the form.

The source explicitly positions eurogames as one hobby-game form among others, alongside wargames, role-playing games, and collectible card games. It treats hobby games as a category with distinct audiences and histories.

## Instruction set

### When the user asks “What is a hobby game?”

Explain that hobby games are usually:

- sought out rather than encountered by accident
- discussed, reviewed, collected, and replayed by enthusiasts
- supported by communities, conventions, or specialist retailers
- designed with repeat play and engaged audiences in mind

Do not reduce the category to difficulty or complexity alone. A hobby game can be accessible. What makes it a hobby game is partly **who it is for** and **how it circulates**.

### When the user asks how eurogames fit in

Explain that eurogames are a later hobby-game form that gained traction by offering:

- cleaner rules than many older hobby formats
- shorter play windows
- reduced elimination and downtime
- less emphasis on direct military conflict
- more emphasis on construction, accumulation, and indirect contest

A related eurogame-design source summarises the archetype with simple rules, one-to-two-hour sessions, low dead time, low elimination, and points-focused competition rather than hard destruction.

### When comparing hobby games to mass-market games

Use practical distinctions:

- **mass-market**: broad distribution, simpler expectations, familiar brands, often one-off or occasional play
- **hobby**: specialist attention, repeated play, design discourse, stronger player identity, and usually a more intentional audience

Do not sneer at mass-market games. Treat them as a different commercial and cultural lane.

## Response patterns Claude should use

### Good explanation pattern

- define the broad category
- place eurogames inside it
- show two or three neighbouring forms
- explain the player culture around those forms

### Weak explanation pattern to avoid

- “Hobby games are just complex board games.”
- “Eurogames replaced everything else.”
- “Mass-market games are bad and hobby games are good.”

## Design relevance

If the user is designing a eurogame, use this subskill to help them answer:

- Who is this for?
- What sort of hobby audience is expected?
- How much rule weight can this audience bear?
- Is the game inviting replay, collection, or community discussion?
