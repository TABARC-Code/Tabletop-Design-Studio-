## Purpose

Use this subskill when the user asks about **competition under social conditions**: how players balance winning with friendliness, how scoring systems affect behaviour, and how a design can stay competitive without poisoning the table.

The source introduction names this explicitly as one of its central questions: how players balance the structure of competitive play with the demands of an intimate social gathering.

## Core principle

In eurogames, people usually want **both**:

- a game worth trying to win
- a social encounter worth having

If the design forces a hard choice between those values too often, it will become socially fragile.

## Design instructions

### 1. Make victory legitimate but not socially toxic

Players should want to win, but the most effective route to victory should not consistently require behaviour the group experiences as petty, humiliating, or joyless.

Good tools:
- constructive point races
- public goals
- scarce but understandable blocking
- transparent denial with opportunity costs

Use caution with:
- repeated theft
- kingmaking traps
- bash-the-leader without restraint
- incentives for prolonged spite when one cannot win

### 2. Design scoring to support emotional clarity

Scoring should:
- be comprehensible during play
- reveal meaningful progress without making the winner obvious too early
- support close races where possible

Bad scoring creates:
- hopeless trailing players
- opaque end states
- post-game confusion about why someone won

### 3. Preserve dignity in defeat

A player who loses should still feel:
- involved
- respected
- interested in what happened
- able to discuss their choices afterwards

Avoid systems where the weakest player spends the last third of the game merely confirming their failure.

## Social-outcome diagnostics

When the user asks why a game causes friction, inspect:

- whether optimal play requires repeated antisocial moves
- whether the game exposes mistakes too harshly in front of friends
- whether one player’s gain feels too directly like another’s personal diminishment
- whether the score arc leaves players emotionally stranded

Recommend sharper competition through:
- tighter scarcity
- more contested turn order
- public objective races
- visible tempo battles

### If the game is too nasty

Recommend softening through:
- replacing theft with blocking
- making denial costlier
- reducing elimination or snowballing
- providing alternate score paths when one route closes

### If the group culture is mixed

Advise the user to design or teach with explicit expectation-setting:
- “This is a tactical blocking game.”
- “This one rewards opportunism but isn’t about bullying.”
- “Aggressive tempo plays are part of the fun here.”

## Best use cases

- fixing sour table dynamics
- tuning score systems
- designing socially durable competition
- advising hosts, teachers, or club organisers
