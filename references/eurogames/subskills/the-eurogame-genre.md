## Purpose

Use this subskill for **practical eurogame design, diagnosis, and refinement**. This is the main mechanism-focused subskill.

The source devotes a full chapter to *The Eurogame Genre*, and the loaded material highlights several important traits: eurogames typically combine simple rulesets, predictable timing, accessible themes, strategic and tactical decision-making, indirect interaction, and a design philosophy that favours collaborative competition over annihilative conflict.

## Core genre traits

When evaluating or designing a eurogame, inspect these axes.

### 1. Rule accessibility

The system should be teachable without a lecture-hall ordeal.

Aim for:
- compact rules overhead
- recurring procedures
- few exception chains
- quick first meaningful turn

### 2. Predictable duration

Eurogames commonly fit a socially manageable session. A related source describes the archetype as games concluded in an hour or two.

### 3. Low dead time

Players should not drift into spectatorship for long. If a role-selection or shared-phase trick can keep everyone mentally present, use it.

### 4. Low elimination and controlled runaway

Prefer endgame scoring contests over early player death. Avoid designs where one early lead becomes mathematically unrecoverable.

### 5. Meaningful decision density

The source stresses that eurogames derive much of their challenge from the decisions produced by player interaction rather than from difficult operational rules.

Therefore, build depth through:
- timing
- opportunity cost
- competition for scarce spaces or cards
- positional sequencing
- engine-shaping trade-offs

### 6. Interaction through contention, not massacre

Indirect interaction is not lesser interaction. In eurogames it is often central.

Use:
- blocking
- racing
- market pressure
- shared incentives
- tempo denial
- action-order competition

Avoid making “take that” the only way the room feels alive.

## Theme guidance

The source shows that history is a frequent thematic backdrop in eurogames, while fantasy appears but science fiction is less dominant. It also shows that theme ranges from deeply integrated to relatively loose, and that players still care about thematic premise, especially when it supports narrative coherence or helps actions make sense.

Use these rules:

- Theme may be light, but it must not confuse incentives.
- If theme is strong, let it support memory and immersion.
- If theme is weak, the mechanisms must still carry emotional texture.
- Do not force simulation fidelity where elegance would serve better.

## Design procedure Claude should follow

### Step 1 — Identify the core choice loop

What repeats every turn?
What scarce thing are players contesting?
What opportunity cost makes the choice painful?

### Step 2 — Identify the scoring logic

How do players convert actions into points, position, or endgame leverage?
If everything converts too smoothly, the game may feel automatic.

### Step 3 — Identify the interaction type

Is it blocking, racing, auctions, drafting, role selection, market tension, map tension, or tempo competition?
If interaction is invisible, ask whether the game will feel solitary.

### Step 4 — Check pacing

- teach time reasonable?
- turn rhythm stable?
- scoring windows legible?
- ending triggered with tension rather than exhaustion?

### Step 5 — Check social resilience

Could a trailing player remain engaged?
Could the table still enjoy the room if someone misplays early?
Could kingmaking or passive-aggressive denial dominate?

## Failure modes and fixes

### Problem: The game feels scripted

Cause:
- one dominant opening
- one obvious action each turn
- weak scarcity

Fix:
- increase contest over spaces/resources
- stagger incentives
- diversify timing windows

### Problem: The game feels multiplayer-solitaire

Cause:
- player boards isolated from each other
- no meaningful shared scarcity
- scoring detached from timing

Fix:
- add shared markets, contested action spaces, auctions, public goals, or role-order pressure

### Problem: Theme feels pasted on

Cause:
- actions make no narrative sense
- title and mechanisms pull in opposite directions

Fix:
- tighten verb choices in rules and components
- align scoring with the fantasy of what players are meant to be doing

### Problem: The game is elegant but bland

Cause:
- decisions are clear but emotionally flat
- too little friction or surprise

Fix:
- add timing tension, variable setup, sharper public races, or lightly differentiated powers

## Strong answer forms for Claude

When helping a user design or fix a eurogame, give:

- one-paragraph genre diagnosis
- bullet list of exact pain points
- a revised choice loop
- one or two alternative interaction structures
- one clear rulebook-facing phrasing suggestion if relevant

## Final reminder

The best eurogame answers the question: **what makes this table interesting every round, with these people, under these rules, in this amount of time?**
