## Purpose

Use this subskill when the user asks **why eurogames are enjoyable**, what kinds of pleasure they produce, or how to tune a game’s feel beyond mechanical adequacy.

The source chapter *The Pleasures of Play* explores enjoyment through strategy, tactics, interaction, theme, and other experiential elements. It notes that eurogames offer mixes of tactical and strategic choice, and that meaningful decisions matter more than raw complexity. It also emphasises that player interaction in eurogames is often indirect, and that theme, even when loosely integrated, can still contribute to enjoyment.

## Pleasure map Claude should use

When diagnosing or designing for pleasure, inspect these sources of delight.

### 1. Strategic pleasure

The slow satisfaction of shaping a plan over time.

Look for:
- engine development
- timing races
- deferred payoff
- positional investment

### 2. Tactical pleasure

The sharp satisfaction of reading the board state and making the right move now.

Look for:
- contested action spaces
- auctions
- role order decisions
- opportunistic blocking

### 3. Interaction pleasure

The source explicitly says eurogames rely on the decisions produced by player interaction and that indirect interaction is central.

Look for:
- “I took the action before you”
- “I priced you out”
- “I forced you to pivot”
- “I read your plan and beat you to the space”

### 4. Thematic pleasure

Players may enjoy a sense of integrated narrative even in eurogames not famous for story. The source records players valuing themes that feel fluidly connected to mechanisms and that allow actions to cohere into small arcs of play.

### 5. Social pleasure

Pleasure is not only in the board state. It is also in:
- teaching well
- table banter
- recognition of smart play
- mutual surprise
- close finishes

## Design rules for increasing pleasure

### Increase pleasure by:

- making choices legible but not trivial
- ensuring every turn changes something visible
- giving players reasons to watch one another
- pacing the game toward a recognisable climax
- letting the theme illuminate decisions without drowning them

### Reduce pleasure by:

- overlong scoring drifts
- hidden dominant strategies
- turns that feel procedurally identical
- interaction so weak players stop caring about others
- interaction so savage players stop enjoying the company

## Pleasure diagnosis prompts

When the user says “the game is fine but flat”, ask:

- Where is the surprise?
- Where is the contest?
- Where is the visible progress?
- Where is the human read of another player’s intention?
- Where is the feeling of having built, secured, or stolen something important?

## Special note on complexity

The source notes that respondents often do not treat complexity itself as highly important. Instead, what matters is whether the game produces meaningful decision-making.

So do not recommend extra mechanisms merely to “add depth.” Depth must cash out as better decisions and stronger table feeling.

## Example recommendation style

When offering improvements, write in this order:

1. what pleasure is missing
2. what mechanism or structure is failing to provide it
3. one concrete change
4. why that change improves the room, not just the rules
