## 1. Genre Definition and Identity Diagnostic

A eurogame is not defined by theme or components, but by design sensibility: constructive competition where players build, develop, optimise, and compare results rather than destroy or eliminate.

### Five-Axis Diagnostic

| Axis | Euro Pole | Non-Euro Pole |
|------|-----------|---------------|
| Player Interaction | Indirect, blocking, racing | Direct attack, removal |
| Conflict Outcome | Comparative scoring | Elimination or collapse |
| Rules Accessibility | Low-to-moderate complexity | High procedural or exception density |
| Playtime | Predictable | Variable or runaway |
| Theme Use | Supports system clarity | Drives rules exceptions |

Classification is gradient, not binary. Use to position a design on the euro spectrum and identify which axes to adjust.

### Calibration levers
- To make a design more euro: reduce elimination, shift to indirect interaction, cap playtime variance, simplify exception density
- To make it less euro: add direct conflict, allow player removal, embrace theme-driven exceptions

## 2. Mechanics and Interaction Taxonomy

Euro mechanics are interaction channels disguised as optimisation systems. They typically generate scarcity competition, positional pressure, tempo pressure, and opportunity denial — rather than destruction or elimination.

### Canonical Euro Mechanics
- Tile Placement
- Auctions
- Trading / Negotiation
- Worker Placement / Role Selection
- Set Collection
- Area Control (indirect form)

### Primary Action Economy Types

| System | What it produces | Typical interaction |
|--------|------------------|---------------------|
| Worker placement | Scarcity + blocking | Denial of preferred actions |
| Action selection | Tempo + opportunity cost | Simultaneous competition for efficiency |
| Auction | Price discovery + valuation | Direct competition for assets |
| Tile/card drafting | Information + denial | Hate-drafting, reading signals |
| Area influence | Positional + majority | Indirect spatial pressure |

### Interaction Intensity Dial
- Minimal: pure engine-building, parallel tracks
- Light: shared markets, indirect blocking
- Medium: contested spaces, auctions, draft denial
- Sharp: mandatory trading, area majority with direct displacement

### Emergent Sub-goals
Euro designs often create emergent sub-goals through: timing windows (when to pivot), positional reads (what opponents are building), and efficiency races (who converts resources faster).

## 3. German Market, Awards, and Diffusion

### Historical Context
- German game market grew strongly through the 1980s–1990s
- Awards culture (Spiel des Jahres, Deutscher Spiele Preis) and Essen/Spiel shaped visibility and design expectations
- International adoption came via enthusiast communities and word-of-mouth, amplified by the late-1990s internet

### Award-Oriented Design Constraints
- Accessibility: teach time under 15 minutes for family; under 30 for hobby
- Predictable session length
- Family-to-hobby crossover friendliness
- Non-elimination, constructive competition

### Diffusion Strategy (for modern releases)
- Community nodes: reviewers, forums, clubs, conventions
- "Rules clarity + table experience" as the marketing engine
- Community-first rather than mass retail push

## 4. Hobbyist Culture and Community

### Core Dynamics
- Eurogames spread through word-of-mouth rather than publisher marketing
- BoardGameGeek functions as the central community hub
- Hobbyists are marked by high engagement: participation, collecting, evangelism

### Hobbyist Persona Archetypes
- **Collector**: acquisition-driven, novelty-seeking, shelf presence matters
- **Optimiser**: seeks depth, replayability, clean decision spaces
- **Evangelist**: teaches games, curates groups, drives adoption

### Design Implications
- Depth and replayability matter more than first-play wow
- Teachability assets (reference cards, quickstart guides) are essential
- Clear decisions that reward skill expression

## 5. Pleasures of Play and Social Outcome Balancing

### Core Principles
- Players tolerate luck if it is not pivotal in determining the winner
- Players value meaningful decisions and strategic planning
- "Chaos" can be perceived randomness emerging from many players' actions

### Pivotality Test for Randomness
Ask: Is luck deciding outcomes, or creating decision texture? If luck determines the winner, redesign. If it creates interesting choices, it's working.

### Chaos Perception
Higher player count + high state volatility = increased "felt randomness" even if mathematical variance is unchanged.

### Randomness Levers
- Draw smoothing (multi-draw, keep-one)
- Predictable refills (open markets)
- Mitigation resources (rerolls, wilds, conversion)
- Timing windows (act before the deck shifts)

### Social Outcome Safeguards
- Avoid kingmaking triggers
- Reduce humiliation loops
- Ensure losing still feels like playing (not waiting)

## 6. Theme-Mechanics Relationship and Cultural Fit

### Core Claims
- Euro sensibility foregrounds mechanics and system, but theme influences adoption and selection
- Theme is not disposable: it supports clarity, memory, and table appeal
- Euro themes often lean toward development, construction, trade, and accumulation

### Theme Selection Workflow
1. Establish the mechanical verbs first (trade, build, allocate, optimise, claim, route, expand, convert)
2. Choose theme that reinforces those verbs
3. Avoid theme-mechanic dissonance (farming theme for a combat game)

### Output Artefacts
- Theme shortlist (3–7 options)
- Component naming map (verb → noun → icon)
- "Theme support" checklist (memory hooks, teachability, tone)

## 7. Interaction Minimalism and Multiplayer Solitaire Diagnosis

### The Problem
Many eurogames use comparative scoring with limited direct-harm mechanics. In extreme cases, players experience this as "multiplayer solitaire" — optimising alone without meaningful response to others.

### Interaction Density Audit
- How often does player A's action change player B's best move?
- How often does player B's action change player A's?
- If the answer is "rarely", interaction is too low.

### Interaction Channels (indirect, no elimination)
- Blocking spaces/actions
- Competing for shared resources
- Tempo / turn-order pressure
- Positional competition (majorities, routes, adjacency)

### Interaction Patch Patterns
- Tighten action slots (fewer, more valuable)
- Make turn order matter (bonus windows, initiative costs)
- Shared markets with visible volatility
- Positional stakes that are readable (majority thresholds, adjacency bonuses)

### Cutthroat Dial Options
- **Friendly**: indirect competition, no denial, parallel engines
- **Medium**: contested spaces, draft denial, shared markets
- **Sharp**: forced trading, area displacement, mandatory interaction
