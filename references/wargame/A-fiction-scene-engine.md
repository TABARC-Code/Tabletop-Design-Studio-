## A §1 — Why Fiction Structure Matters to Wargame Design

Wargame scenarios, campaign chapters, and in-game dramatic moments are not merely mechanical exercises — they are narrative events. Players remember them as stories. The question is whether the designer shaped that story intentionally or let it emerge accidentally.

Understanding scene and sequel structure allows designers to:
- Construct scenarios with built-in dramatic arcs
- Write campaign chapters that feel like novel chapters
- Build activation systems that mirror narrative pacing (→ D)
- Design victory conditions that produce satisfying story conclusions (→ C §6)
- Write rules text that teaches through narrative examples (→ R)

**Core principle:** The table is a stage. The rules are the playwright's constraints. The players are the actors. The designer's job is to make every constraint produce drama.

## A §2 — The Scene-Sequel Engine (Bickham)

Bickham's model is the most actionable framework for constructing narrative moments in any medium. It applies directly to tabletop scenario design.

### A §2.1 — The Scene

A scene is a unit of conflict. It has three mandatory components:

| Component | Definition | In wargame terms |
|-----------|-----------|-----------------|
| **Goal** | What the viewpoint character (player) is trying to achieve | The scenario objective: "Hold the bridge," "Assassinate the target," "Extract the artefact" |
| **Conflict** | The obstacle or opposition preventing achievement | The opposing force, the terrain, the time limit, the dice |
| **Disaster** | The scene ends in failure, partial failure, or a new complication — never clean success | The scenario does not end with "and everything worked out" — it ends with a changed situation |

**The rule:** Scenes must not end in unqualified success. Clean wins kill narrative momentum. The scene ends with the goal achieved but a new problem created, OR the goal partially achieved, OR the goal failed with a consequence.

**Applied to scenario design:**
- A mission where the player achieves everything perfectly with no cost is a bad scenario
- Every victory condition should produce a new question: "What do we do now?"
- Pyrrhic victories are often more narratively satisfying than clean ones

→ Cross-load G §6: Inquisitor scenarios built around divergent motivations and timed events are structured around this principle.
→ Cross-load E §6: the tempo curve (turns 1–4) maps directly onto Goal → Conflict → Disaster.

---

### A §2.2 — The Sequel

The sequel is the emotional and intellectual response to a scene disaster. It also has three mandatory components:

| Component | Definition | In wargame terms |
|-----------|-----------|-----------------|
| **Reaction** | Emotional response to the disaster | The players process what just happened ("We lost the objective," "The lieutenant is down") |
| **Dilemma** | A genuine choice between two bad options | "Do we push for the artefact and risk the warband, or retreat and let them have it?" |
| **Decision** | The choice made, which creates the next scene's goal | The player commits: "We push." This becomes the new goal. |

**The rule:** A dilemma must be genuine — both options must be costly. A choice between a good option and a bad option is not a dilemma; it is a test. A choice between two bad options is drama.

**Applied to campaign design:**
- Between-session choices are sequels: the player reacts to the last game's disaster, faces a dilemma, makes a decision
- Campaign branching points are formalised dilemmas
- The best campaign moments are when the player makes a decision they cannot take back

→ Cross-load G §7: Inquisitor campaign architecture (permanent injuries, moral corruption) creates genuine dilemmas between sessions.
→ Cross-load F §7: narrative campaign structures — branching quests are chains of scene-sequel loops.

---

### A §2.3 — The Full Loop

```
SCENE:    Goal → Conflict → Disaster
               ↓
SEQUEL:   Reaction → Dilemma → Decision
               ↓
SCENE:    [New Goal from Decision] → [New Conflict] → [New Disaster]
               ↓
          [Repeat until resolution]
```

**A wargame session is 1–3 scene-sequel loops.** A campaign is 6–20.

The loop accelerates toward a climax. Early scenes: goals are clear, disasters are recoverable. Late scenes: goals are desperate, disasters are permanent.

→ Cross-load E §6: the optimal small-format tempo curve (turns 1–4) is a compressed scene-sequel loop.
→ Cross-load D §6: turn 1 engineering ensures the first "disaster" is recoverable — the loop has room to run.

## A §3 — Scene Types for Wargame Designers

Not all dramatic moments in a wargame are the same. Identify which type you are designing before writing the mechanics or the scenario.

| Scene Type | Description | Design Expression |
|-----------|-------------|------------------|
| **Action scene** | Physical conflict with clear stakes | Combat mechanics, lethality curves, activation flow |
| **Investigation scene** | Gathering information under pressure | Hidden objectives, secret information, GM-revealed clues (→ G §5) |
| **Decision scene** | Two or more parties confront a genuine dilemma | Branching scenario outcomes, player-choice consequence tables |
| **Reversal scene** | The situation flips — the advantaged becomes disadvantaged | Twist cards (→ F §5), sudden objective shifts, reinforcement arrivals |
| **Climax scene** | The final confrontation; all earlier threads resolve | Turn 4–5 in a skirmish; the campaign's final session |
| **Aftermath scene** | The consequences of victory or defeat | Post-game tables, injury rolls, campaign progression sequences |

**Design rule:** A session should contain at least two different scene types. Pure action without investigation, reversal, or aftermath is mechanically interesting but narratively thin.

→ Cross-load G §6: Inquisitor scenario design covers investigation and decision scenes comprehensively.
→ Cross-load C §6: scenario design from the macro-theory perspective addresses action and climax scenes.

## A §4 — Tension & Release Architecture

Drama requires alternating tension and release. Without release, tension becomes numbing. Without tension, release is meaningless.

### A §4.1 — Tension Sources in Wargames
- Activation uncertainty (who goes next? → D)
- Dice swings on crucial rolls (will the leader survive?)
- Objective pressure (time running out; score shifting)
- Narrative stakes (campaign consequences looming)
- Resource scarcity (last command point, last model that can contest)

### A §4.2 — Release Sources in Wargames
- A high-risk action succeeding spectacularly
- An unexpected reversal favoring the losing player (→ E §4)
- A moment of comedy or absurdity in the dice
- A character surviving against the odds
- A scenario ending clearly (not fizzling out)

### A §4.3 — Pacing the Tension Curve
The ideal pattern across a session:

```
Low tension (setup) → Rising tension (first engagement) → 
Peak tension (crisis turn) → Brief release → 
Final tension (resolution) → Clear conclusion
```

This mirrors the scene-sequel loop and the tempo curve from E §6.

**Warning:** Avoid sustained maximum tension. Players need micro-releases to stay emotionally available for the peak moments. Unrelenting pressure is numbing, not dramatic.

→ Cross-load E §4.6: tempo-level negative feedback creates intentional release moments at turn 1.
→ Cross-load D §10: activation timing as emotional rhythm — the tools are the same as tension architecture.

## A §5 — Dramatic Focus

Every scenario needs a dramatic focus — a single question that the session is built around answering.

Examples:
- "Can the warband escape before the trap closes?"
- "Who gets to the artefact first?"
- "Does the inquisitor trust the informant?"
- "Can a broken force hold the line for one more turn?"

The dramatic focus is not the victory condition. It is the emotional question underlying the mechanical contest. A good victory condition answers the dramatic focus question through play rather than through a text resolution.

**Test:** Can you state the session's dramatic focus in one sentence? If not, the scenario lacks identity.

→ Cross-load G §6: Inquisitor scenarios require a strong narrative hook — this is the dramatic focus stated as premise.
→ Cross-load F §1: Warcry's design philosophy — "fun, surprising, story-rich moments should happen by default" — is a commitment to emergent dramatic focus.

## A §6 — Writing In-Universe Fiction (Scenario Intros, Campaign Text, Faction Lore)

When writing fiction for wargame books — scenario intros, campaign chapter openings, faction background — apply these principles:

### A §6.1 — Opening in Scene
Never open in exposition. Open in action, in a specific moment, with a specific character making a specific decision. The world-building comes second.

Bad: *"The hive city of Mordgrave had been contested for three hundred years. Its shadowed streets…"*
Better: *"Mira pressed her back to the corroded bulkhead and counted shots. Four rounds left. Eight of them."*

### A §6.2 — Keep POV Tight
In-universe wargame fiction is most effective when written from a single, limited perspective. Omniscient narration feels like a history lesson. A character's perspective creates immersion.

### A §6.3 — End on a Hook
Scenario fiction should end at the moment of crisis — the instant before the players take control. The last line should make the player want to pick up their dice.

## A §7 — Scene Structure Applied to Rules Examples

Rules examples are mini-scenes. They should follow scene logic.

A bad example: *"If a unit is within 6" of an objective, it scores 1 point."*
(This is a definition, not an example.)

A good example: *"Kira's Stormwatch is 5" from the shrine — just within range. She scores 1 point. But a Rot-Cult Fanatic is charging from the north. Can she hold it?"*

The good example: establishes a goal (hold the shrine), implies conflict (the charging enemy), and creates a micro-disaster question (can she hold?). It teaches the rule and establishes dramatic context simultaneously.

**Rule:** Write every complex rules example as a mini-scene with a goal and a conflict. It teaches faster, sticks longer, and makes the book a pleasure to read.

→ Cross-load R §3.1: sentence labelling — examples are a defined category in the forensic audit.
→ Cross-load R §2.3: tone selection — narrative examples can carry voice that the procedure body cannot.

## A §8 — Scene-Sequel Mapping for Campaign Designers

When designing a campaign, map the full arc before writing individual sessions:

```
SESSION 1:  Introductory scene — establish goal, introduce conflict, minor disaster
SESSION 2:  Escalation scene — complications compound; a character is endangered
SESSION 3:  Midpoint reversal — unexpected shift; player choices matter
SESSION 4:  Crisis scene — stakes are maximum; permanent consequences possible
SESSION 5:  Climax — dramatic focus answered; the question is resolved
AFTERMATH:  Sequel — reactions, dilemmas, next campaign arc seeds
```

Each session is a scene. The full campaign is a sequel chain. The final session should feel like the answer to a question asked in session 1.

**Design rule:** Write the climax session first. Then work backwards to plant the seeds.

→ Cross-load G §7: Inquisitor campaign architecture — permanent consequences and corruption arcs map onto this structure.
→ Cross-load F §7: Warcry-style campaign structures with branching quests are lighter implementations of the same arc.

## A §9 — Failure Modes in Narrative Design

| Problem | Cause | Fix |
|---------|-------|-----|
| Sessions feel disposable | No scene-sequel continuity; each game is self-contained | Embed campaign consequences; add aftermath tables |
| Climax feels anticlimactic | Climax scene lacks a genuine dilemma | Ensure the final decision costs something regardless of outcome |
| Players disengage mid-game | Tension plateau without release | Add a reversal event or twist at the midpoint (→ F §5 twist cards) |
| Scenario feels random | No dramatic focus; unclear what the session is "about" | State the dramatic focus in one sentence before designing mechanics |
| Fiction text feels inert | Opens in exposition; no scene logic | Apply A §6.1 — open in action, close on a hook |
| Rules examples don't teach | Written as definitions, not mini-scenes | Apply A §7 — goal + conflict + implied question |
