## E §1 — Core Reality: Small Format = High Volatility

Small-format games (8–25 models per side) amplify every design flaw:

| Amplified Risk | Why It's Worse at Small Scale |
|----------------|-------------------------------|
| Variance / spiky dice | One bad roll = 12% of your army |
| Alpha strikes | Losing 2 models early = losing 25% of force |
| Activation asymmetry | Each extra activation is proportionally more powerful |
| Snowballing | Early lead compounds with fewer models left to contest it |
| Frustration curve | Players feel helpless if down 2–3 models by turn 2 |

**Therefore:** Small-format wargames require heavier structural correction than mass battles. Negative feedback is not charity for the loser — it is necessary scaffolding.

→ Cross-load D §1: activation system choice is the first structural decision affecting all these risks.
→ Cross-load B §4–5: damage and survivability curves must be deliberately smoother in small format.

## E §2 — What Is a Negative Feedback Loop?

A negative feedback loop:
- Helps the disadvantaged player re-enter competitiveness
- Prevents runaway victories
- Supports dramatic pacing
- Makes every decision feel meaningful
- Ensures the game is not decided by turn 1

**Negative feedback is not rubber-banding if it is:**
- Transparent and predictable
- Thematically coherent (the underdog "rallies," the winner "overextends")
- Mild enough not to punish the winning player
- Embedded in the system rather than bolted on externally

Types: Mechanical | Scenario-based | Activation-based | Environmental | Resource-based

→ Cross-load C §13: player psychology — comeback possibility is essential to perceived fairness.

## E §3 — The Spearhead Model (Modern Small-Format Blueprint)

Derived from Warhammer: Spearhead designer notes. A robust blueprint for quick-play, small-format systems.

### E §3.1 — Fixed Army Lists
No freeform list building. Pre-balanced detachments.
- Removes point-optimisation exploits at source
- Levels the playing field before the first model is placed
- Reduces barrier to entry (no list-building knowledge required)

### E §3.2 — Short Game Length (4 Rounds)
- Prevents runaway advantages from compounding
- Keeps tempo tight — every round has to matter
- Ensures mid-game (turns 2–3) is always the decisive zone

### E §3.3 — Active Negative Feedback
- Underperforming player gains mission twists, positional advantages, or scoring boosts
- Feedback is embedded in the scenario structure, not added as a pity rule

### E §3.4 — Environmental & Mission Pressure
- Objectives force forward movement
- Forward movement reduces alpha-strike gunline viability
- The scenario itself is the negative feedback mechanism

→ Cross-load C §6: scenario design as the primary negative feedback vehicle.
→ Cross-load D §6.1: turn 1 engineering works in tandem with the Spearhead model.

## E §4 — Master Toolkit: Negative Feedback Mechanisms

### E §4.1 — Underdog Boosts (Simple, Transparent)
- Losing player chooses first activator next round
- Free re-roll once per turn for the player behind
- Slight movement bonus when below half strength
- Extra command point generation when losing
- Advantage on tiebreaks and priority ties

Must be: mild, predictable, thematically consistent, never feel like cheating.

### E §4.2 — Rubber-Banding Without Feeling Artificial
The trick: embed feedback in the scenario, not in a pity rule.

Tools:
- Twist cards that favour the player who is behind
- Objectives that change value mid-game
- Environmental escalation (storms, reinforcements) triggered by game state
- Delayed scoring windows that the losing player can reach

### E §4.3 — Scenario-Level Negative Feedback (Most Elegant)
Built into the mission itself:
- Player behind gets priority on objective flips
- Mid-board objectives worth more in later turns (trailing player has more time to reach them)
- Scoring escalates over time, closing the gap if both players contest actively
- Deployment advantage for the player who lost last game (campaign)

**This is the most invisible and effective tool.** The mission protects the losing player without a single explicit rule doing so.

→ Cross-load C §6: scenario design with the "why engage?" audit.
→ Cross-load F §5: modular scenario card engines automatically introduce variety into this system.

### E §4.4 — Activation-Level Negative Feedback
Critical in Alternate Activation systems:
- Once-per-round unactivated reaction for the losing side
- "Second wind" activation available when outnumbered
- Reduced penalty on forced activations for the smaller warband
- Priority roll bias weighted toward the underdog

→ Cross-load D §4.4: AA stabilisation tools in the activation module.
→ Cross-load F §3: Warcry's specific lopsided-activation flaw and solutions.

### E §4.5 — Unit-Level Negative Feedback
Use sparingly:
- Wounded units gain desperation abilities
- Losses unlock faction-wide surge effects
- Fearless or frenzied state triggers below 25% strength
- Temporary stat boosts based on cumulative casualties taken

**Warning:** Too much unit-level feedback feels artificial. Reserve for thematic moments (last stand) rather than standard gameplay.

### E §4.6 — Tempo-Level Negative Feedback
Control the battle's lethality rhythm:
- Turn 1 ranged attacks deal reduced damage (–1 or half)
- No charge actions permitted on turn 1
- Special abilities and Tier 3–4 rules locked until turn 2+
- Staggered reserve arrivals (models enter play in waves)
- Objective value increases progressively

All these reduce destructive potential of early activations, guaranteeing the mid-game matters.

→ Cross-load D §6: turn 1 engineering from the activation module perspective.
→ Cross-load C §12: pace and tempo engineering from macro theory.

### E §4.7 — Resource-Level Negative Feedback
- Fewer models = more command dice / tokens per model
- Smaller board-control footprint triggers comeback resource generation
- Losing player gets improved draw odds from ability decks
- Reinforcement tokens available only after reaching a casualty threshold

Works well with: token economies, card engines, dice pool systems.

→ Cross-load F §2: Warcry dice economy as a built-in resource-level feedback system.

### E §4.8 — Hidden Negative Feedback (Environmental)
These don't feel like rules — they feel like "world realities":
- Terrain density naturally provides cover to the losing player's remaining models
- Threat ranges too short for instant full-force deletion
- High HP pools discouraging alpha strike efficiency
- Mandatory movement rules spreading out models (reducing concentration)
- Turn structure limiting multi-combo activation spikes

**These are the most elegant tools.** Players never notice them operating.

→ Cross-load C §11: table geometry as silent negative feedback.
→ Cross-load B §5: survivability curve design as a hidden feedback mechanism.

## E §5 — Critical Small-Format Problems & Solutions

### E §5.1 — Alpha Strikes (Primary Problem)
The #1 design failure in small-format games.

Fixes:
- Restrict turn 1 shooting ranges to under half-table
- Block LOS heavily with terrain in deployment zones
- Deploy in staggered positions (no model in the open)
- Introduce reaction windows in the defender's turn
- Prohibit charge + shoot combo on turn 1
- Apply scenario shields (protected zones in early turns)

→ Cross-load D §3.4: IGOUGO-specific alpha strike mitigation.
→ Cross-load C §11: terrain as alpha strike prevention.

### E §5.2 — Skew Lists (If List Building Exists)
All-melee rush, all-ranged gunline, monster spam, horde-padding.

Fixes:
- Limit unit duplication (max 2 of any unit)
- Require mandatory role slots in list construction
- Apply soft caps via point weighting on duplicates
- Hard keyword limits (max 2 units with same keyword)

→ Cross-load B §8: category cap models in the points system.

### E §5.3 — Lopsided Activations (AA Flaw)
Fix via: Kill Team second-activation penalty, activation passing with tokens, random draw elements to break lock-step, detachment-based activation.

→ Cross-load D §4.4: AA stabilisation tools.

### E §5.4 — Spike Damage / Too Swingy
Fix via:
- Cap critical hit multiplier (crits deal +1, never ×3)
- Gate crits behind a resource token (must spend to trigger)
- Increase base defence across all profiles
- Introduce "steady damage" minimums so even bad rolls do something
- Mandatory re-rolls of extreme outlier results (optional rule for casual play)

→ Cross-load B §4: damage curve tuning for controlled output.

### E §5.5 — Overcomplexity Creep
Small-format games demand simplicity because every rule must be manageable for every model.

Fix via:
- Hard cap: 3 special rules per unit maximum
- One keyword per function — no multi-function keywords
- Minimal bookkeeping (avoid per-model token stacks)
- Single-layered abilities only (no "if A is true when B triggers, then C unless D")

→ Cross-load B §6: special rules economy.
→ Cross-load R §2: rules writing style for small-format clarity.

## E §6 — Optimal Small-Format Tempo Curve

The ideal dramatic arc for a 4–5 turn skirmish:

| Turn | Name | Lethality | Focus |
|------|------|-----------|-------|
| 1 | Position | Low | Movement; deployment optimisation; threat staging |
| 2 | First Engagement | Medium | Controlled early combat; stakes rise |
| 3 | Crisis | High | Game-pivoting decisions; big swings justified |
| 4 | Resolution | Climactic | Scores crystallise; winner emerges |
| 5 | Climax (optional) | Varies | Campaign use; dramatic last stands |

This arc matches: Bickham's scene-sequel pacing model, Spearhead's 4-round structure, and modern competitive skirmish patterns.
