## I §1 — The Core Fantasy (Your "You Are…" Statement)

Every wargame universe begins with a single anchoring statement — the game's core fantasy. This is not lore. It is the player's role within the fiction.

**Structure:** "You are [role], [doing what], [in what world]."

| Game | Core Fantasy |
|------|-------------|
| Warhammer 40,000 | You are a general commanding the remnants of humanity against extinction, in a dying galaxy crushed by its own cruel gods |
| Malifaux | You are a crime boss and duelist, pulling strings in a nightmare frontier city where death is just the beginning |
| Frostgrave | You are a wizard-explorer, risking your warband to plunder a frozen dead city before your rivals do |
| A hypothetical historical game | You are a field commander on the Eastern Front, making decisions with incomplete information as the temperature drops |

**The core fantasy does three things:**
1. Gives players an immediate sense of identity and agency
2. Establishes the emotional register of the setting
3. Constrains faction design — every faction must make sense within the fantasy

→ Cross-load C §1: "What kind of battle does this game portray?" is answered by the core fantasy.
→ Cross-load G §1.3: in Inquisitor-style games, the core fantasy becomes "You are this specific character."
→ Cross-load H: the core fantasy's tone determines which persona blend is appropriate for all design work.

## I §2 — World Architecture Layers

A wargame world is built in layers. Design from the outside in, but reveal from the inside out (players experience the personal layer first; the cosmic layer is background).

```
COSMIC LAYER    — The metaphysical rules of the universe: gods, warp, magic, physics
      ↓
CIVILISATIONAL  — Empires, factions, major historical events, current wars
      ↓
POLITICAL       — Power structures within and between factions; doctrine; ideology
      ↓
OPERATIONAL     — The specific conflict the game is set within; what is at stake
      ↓
PERSONAL        — Individual characters, warbands, squads; the player's direct perspective
```

Players live at the Personal and Operational layers. The Civilisational and Political layers create context and stakes. The Cosmic layer creates the feeling that the world is larger than the game.

**Design error:** Building the cosmic layer in detail before knowing what the personal layer needs. Start at the personal layer and build upward only as needed.

→ Cross-load H §2.6 (Leon Stratton): for cosmic layer design.
→ Cross-load H §2.7 (Dr. Cassian Veyr): for hard-SF constraint on the cosmic layer.
→ Cross-load H §2.8 (Professor Aldous Thorne): for civilisational and political layer design.
→ Cross-load H §2.9 (Dr. Lucian Graves): for ideology and power structure at the political layer.

## I §3 — Faction Design Framework

Factions are the primary vehicle for player identity and world-building in a wargame. A faction is not a collection of stats — it is a coherent argument for a way of being in the world.

### I §3.1 — The Faction Identity Stack

Every faction needs:

| Layer | Question | Example (40K Space Marines) |
|-------|----------|----------------------------|
| **Ideology** | What does this faction believe, want, and fear? | Humanity's survival through martial superiority and devotion |
| **Doctrine** | How does this ideology express itself in warfare? | Shock assault, elite units, combined arms, courage under fire |
| **Aesthetic** | What does this faction look and sound like? | Gothic power armour, bolter fire, battle-hymns |
| **Mechanical identity** | What does this faction do on the table that no other faction does? | High individual model quality; resilient; flexible army construction |
| **Weakness profile** | What is this faction's structural vulnerability? | Expensive; few models; can be overwhelmed by attrition |
| **Narrative role** | What story does this faction tell in the universe? | Humanity's guardians; heroes with feet of clay; tragic devotion |

**Design rule:** If two factions have the same ideology, different aesthetics, and the same mechanical identity, one of them is redundant. Factions must be distinct at all six layers.

→ Cross-load B §10: faction asymmetry — the faction identity stack is what asymmetry expresses.
→ Cross-load C §2 (top-down design): faction design is top-down — ideology → doctrine → mechanics.

---

### I §3.2 — The War Rationale

Every conflict in a wargame needs a war rationale — the reason these factions are fighting, right now, on this table.

The war rationale must be:
- **Comprehensible** — players understand why these forces are fighting
- **Symmetrical in stakes** — both sides have something genuine to win or lose
- **Morally ambiguous** (optional but powerful) — neither faction is simply "the bad guys"

War rationale types:

| Type | Description | Example |
|------|-------------|---------|
| **Survival** | One or both factions will cease to exist if they lose | Hive Fleet Leviathan vs. humanity |
| **Territory** | Control of a resource, location, or strategic position | Contested frontier world |
| **Ideology** | One faction's existence is a threat to another's worldview | Inquisitor: radical vs puritan |
| **Artefact** | Both factions want the same object | Frostgrave artefact hunting |
| **Revenge / honour** | Historical grievance driving present conflict | Clan warfare, blood feuds |
| **Proxy war** | Factions are proxies for a larger conflict above them | Mercenaries fighting for distant masters |

→ Cross-load A §2.1: the war rationale is the macro-level "goal" in the scene-sequel structure.
→ Cross-load G §6: Inquisitor scenarios need divergent motivations — multiple war rationales operating simultaneously.

## I §4 — Setting Construction

Setting is the specific place and time of the conflict. It is distinct from world-building — the setting is where the game happens; the world is everything else.

### I §4.1 — Setting Variables

| Variable | Controls |
|----------|---------|
| **Scale** | Planetary, continental, city, district, single building |
| **Era** | Historical period, technological level, societal stage |
| **Density** | Urban, wilderness, space, underground, aquatic |
| **Tone** | Gothic, heroic, grimdark, hopeful, satirical, tragic |
| **Stability** | Settled frontier, war zone, post-apocalypse, active conflict |

A setting is a specific intersection of these variables. "Grimdark urban war zone in a dying hive city" is a setting. "The future" is not.

### I §4.2 — Mechanical Setting Integration

Setting is not decoration — it has mechanical implications:

| Setting Element | Mechanical Impact |
|----------------|------------------|
| Dense urban terrain | Short LOS; complex movement; AA activation suits better than IGOUGO |
| Open wilderness | Long-range dominance risk; terrain placement critical |
| Subterranean / tunnels | No long-range weapons; surprise encounters; tight scenarios |
| Zero-gravity / space | Custom movement rules; no elevation advantage |
| Active hazards (storms, fire, radiation) | Environmental damage tables; forced movement |

**Design rule:** Every setting choice that has aesthetic importance should also have a mechanical expression. If it doesn't affect how the game is played, it is irrelevant to design.

→ Cross-load C §11: table geometry and movement economy as setting expression.
→ Cross-load B §9: terrain design as the physical translation of setting.

## I §5 — Cosmological Architecture

The cosmological layer gives the universe its metaphysical rules — the laws that govern existence beyond normal physics. This layer is optional for historical games but essential for fantasy and SF universes.

### I §5.1 — Cosmological Systems

| System Type | Controls |
|-------------|---------|
| **Magic / psychic power** | What can be done beyond normal physics; who can do it; what it costs |
| **Divine presence** | Are gods real in the world? Do they intervene? What do they want? |
| **Death and afterlife** | Is death permanent? What happens to souls? Can the dead return? |
| **Technology ceiling** | What technology exists? What has been lost? What is forbidden? |
| **Cosmological threat** | Is there an existential danger larger than any faction? |

### I §5.2 — Cosmological Coherence Test

Every element of the cosmological layer must pass:
1. **Internal consistency** — the rules of the universe don't contradict themselves
2. **Player impact** — the cosmological layer affects what players do on the table (even if only through faction abilities or scenario stakes)
3. **Mystery preservation** — not everything is explained; the best cosmologies have active questions at their centre

→ Cross-load H §2.6 (Leon Stratton): mythopoetic cosmology with mythic resonance.
→ Cross-load H §2.7 (Dr. Cassian Veyr): hard-SF constraints on cosmological plausibility.

## I §6 — Civilisational & Political Architecture

The civilisational layer gives factions their context. Players don't just fight for a faction — they fight for a position within a larger political reality.

### I §6.1 — Civilisational Design

| Element | Questions to Answer |
|---------|-------------------|
| **Power structure** | Who rules? By what authority? Who challenges that authority? |
| **Economy** | What do factions compete for? What creates scarcity? What is the war economy? |
| **Technology** | What can each faction build? What do they lack? Who controls key technologies? |
| **Culture** | What does each faction believe about warfare, honour, death, and sacrifice? |
| **History** | What past events created the current conflict? What grievances are unresolved? |

→ Cross-load H §2.8 (Professor Aldous Thorne): for structured civilisational modelling.
→ Cross-load H §2.9 (Dr. Lucian Graves): for faction ideology and power-structure analysis.

### I §6.2 — Political Architecture

Politics within and between factions creates narrative depth and scenario variety:

- **Internal factionalism:** Not all members of a faction agree — radicals vs puritans (→ G §8), reformers vs traditionalists, old guard vs new blood
- **Alliance structures:** Who works with whom, and why? What are the conditions of alliance? When does it break?
- **Intelligence and deception:** Who knows what? Who is hiding what? How does information asymmetry create tactical and narrative tension?
- **Shifting loyalties:** Can units change sides? Can mercenaries be hired? What are the campaign consequences?

→ Cross-load G §8: Inquisitor's radical vs puritan conflict is a masterclass in internal faction politics as game content.
→ Cross-load A §2.2 (sequel — dilemma): political dilemmas between sessions are some of the most powerful sequel moments in campaign design.

## I §7 — The Operational Layer: War Contexts

The operational layer is the specific conflict the game is set within. This is the layer closest to the table.

### I §7.1 — War Context Types

| Context | Description | Scenario Implication |
|---------|-------------|---------------------|
| **Open battle** | Two forces meet in the field with intent to destroy or route | Standard symmetrical scenarios; IGOUGO or AA both viable |
| **Raid / strike** | One force attacks a specific target held by another | Asymmetrical scenarios; defender has positional advantage |
| **Siege** | Attacker must breach; defender must hold | Long-form scenarios; reinforcement timing critical |
| **Patrol / skirmish** | Small forces encounter each other unexpectedly | Small-format; alternating activation; fog of war |
| **Pursuit** | Attacker chases a retreating force | Movement-heavy; time pressure; escape objectives |
| **Assassination / objective seizure** | One or both forces target specific characters or items | Character-centric; Inquisitor-style; hidden objectives |
| **Reconnaissance** | Information gathering under fire | Hidden information mechanics; non-lethal objectives |

→ Cross-load C §6: scenario design addresses how war context shapes mission structure.
→ Cross-load F §5: modular scenario card engines represent war contexts through deck composition.

## I §8 — World-Building in Rules Text

Setting and world are present in rules text even when not explicitly described. Every mechanical choice carries a world-building implication.

| Mechanical Choice | World-Building Statement |
|------------------|------------------------|
| Models have souls / morale breaks | Soldiers are human; they can be broken |
| Models never flee; only die | This faction is inhuman, fanatical, or mechanical |
| Magic is unreliable / tests required | Power has a cost; the universe resists manipulation |
| Command range limits | Command structures are fragile under fire |
| Weapon descriptions use real terms | Historical authenticity matters; the fiction is grounded |
| Weapon descriptions use invented terms | This is a fully fictional universe; novelty is valued |

**Design principle:** Make every mechanical choice consistent with the world's internal logic. Players should be able to infer setting from mechanics even without reading a single word of fiction.

→ Cross-load C §9: weapon systems design as faction identity and world-building.
→ Cross-load R §2.3: tone selection in rules text carries world-building weight even in clinical language.

## I §9 — The Overall Conceit (Ford & Hutchinson)

Ford and Hutchinson identify the "overall conceit" as the collection of narrative devices explaining the game world and the player's place and motivation within it.

**The "you are…" statement is the conceit's core.** Everything else — setting, factions, scenarios, campaigns — supports and expands that conceit.

**The conceit must:**
1. Provide immediate entry into the fiction (players know what they are before they read the rules)
2. Explain why conflict is happening (the war rationale is embedded)
3. Support many possible stories (the conceit is a framework, not a script)
4. Invite player ownership (players extend the conceit through their force, their characters, their campaigns)

→ Cross-load F §1: Warcry's conceit is embedded in its design — "warbands competing for the favour of dark gods in the savage Bloodwind Spoil."
→ Cross-load A §5: the dramatic focus of each session is a specific expression of the overall conceit.

## I §10 — Genre-Specific Design Notes

### I §10.1 — Historical Wargames
- Accuracy creates accountability: if you misrepresent a weapon's performance, the audience will know
- The war rationale is given by history; the designer's job is making it emotionally legible
- Faction asymmetry is historical fact, not designer choice
- The moral weight of historical conflicts must be handled with care and intentionality

→ Cross-load H §2.3 (Gregory Harker): for historically grounded analytical exposition.

### I §10.2 — Fantasy Wargames
- Magic systems must have internal consistency (→ I §5)
- Mythology and cosmology create faction depth beyond "elves vs dwarves"
- The "secondary world" must feel like it existed before the game began

→ Cross-load H §2.6 (Leon Stratton): for mythopoetic fantasy world-building.

### I §10.3 — Science Fiction Wargames
- Technology must be differentiated — factions need distinct tech philosophies, not just different weapon names
- Hard-SF worldbuilding constraints increase immersion for technical audiences
- The gap between current reality and the game's future creates setting tone

→ Cross-load H §2.7 (Dr. Cassian Veyr): for hard-SF technical grounding.

### I §10.4 — Narrative / Grimdark
- Moral ambiguity is a feature, not a failure — design factions with genuine virtues and genuine crimes
- Tone is established through faction history and doctrine, not just visual design
- The setting should feel like it preceded the player and will outlast them

→ Cross-load H §2.9 (Dr. Lucian Graves): for ideological and sociological depth.
→ Cross-load G §8: Inquisitor's thematic pillars as a masterclass in grimdark setting design.

## I §11 — World & Cosmos Design Checklist

**Core Fantasy**
- [ ] Is there a single "You are…" statement that defines the player's role?
- [ ] Does the core fantasy establish emotional register and constrain faction design?

**Factions**
- [ ] Does each faction have a distinct ideology, doctrine, aesthetic, and mechanical identity?
- [ ] Does each faction have a built-in weakness profile?
- [ ] Is the war rationale between factions clear and symmetrical in stakes?

**Setting**
- [ ] Is the setting specific at each world-architecture layer?
- [ ] Does every significant setting choice have a mechanical expression?

**Cosmology (if applicable)**
- [ ] Does the cosmological system pass the internal consistency test?
- [ ] Does it affect what players do on the table?
- [ ] Does it preserve active mystery?

**Political layer**
- [ ] Are there internal divisions within factions available for scenario use?
- [ ] Is there an intelligence / information asymmetry dimension?
