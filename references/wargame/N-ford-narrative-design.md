# N — Ford & Hutchinson: Narrative Design

Overall and minor conceits, embedded vs emergent narrative, dramatic focus (objective vs subjective viewpoint), designing for emergent chaos, and narrative mechanics in miniatures games.

**Primary source:** Glenn Ford & Mike Hutchinson — *The Fundamentals of Tabletop Miniatures Game Design* (CRC, 2025) — Chapters 9–10

## N §1 — The Overall Conceit

Ford and Hutchinson define the overall conceit as the collection of narrative devices that explain the game world and the player's place and motivation within it.

At its core: the "you are…" statement.

**What the overall conceit does:**
1. Provides the overall framing for what the player is trying to achieve and how
2. Gives the player an immediate entry point into the fiction
3. Guides their expectations and interpretation of all rules

**Conceit for omniscient viewpoint:** Non-discrete miniatures games present players with an omniscient view — you see the entire battlefield at once. This contradicts the "you are a general commanding from the front" conceit. Solutions:
- MacGuffins: "you are in a command bunker with a heads-up display"
- Rules-based interference: fog-of-war mechanics that limit what the "general" officially knows

**Communicate it early and loudly.** The overall conceit is the first and most important step in helping the player establish the conceptual framework through which they will interpret every rule.

## N §2 — Minor Conceits

Minor conceits are found throughout a game as metaphors that imbue abstract mechanical actions with narrative meaning.

Without them, rules would be bafflingly opaque.

**Example:** In Warhammer Fantasy Battles, the sequence "roll to hit → roll to wound → roll to save" is, mechanically, three abstract dice checks comparing numerical statistics. The minor conceits of "weapon skill," "strength," "toughness," "armour value" frame each roll as a moment in a warrior swinging a sword at an armoured opponent.

**Design application:** Where in-world logic of a rule is not immediately obvious, add brief commentary before the mechanical description. This helps the player integrate the rule into their conceptual framework, aiding both comprehension and recall.

→ Cross-load R §2.3: tone — minor conceits are the thematic layer within technical rules text.
→ Cross-load A §7: rules examples as mini-scenes — the same principle expressed as narrative structure.

## N §3 — Embedded Narrative

Embedded narrative elements are placed explicitly into the game by the designer.

**Forms:**
- Backstory and setting descriptions
- Scenario introductions with characters and motivations
- Unit descriptions (force-building guidance)
- Descriptions of weapons, terrain, and special rules framed in-world

**Strengths:**
- Simple for players; predictable for designers
- Essential entry point — without it, players have nothing to pull them into the fiction
- Specific details act as triggers for player imagination to build on

**Risk:** Too much embedded narrative can make players feel like actors in the designer's story rather than the authors of their own.

**Practical principles:**

*Context, not control.* Embedded narrative works best when it provides context and framing, not conclusions. Provide backstory; then let go of the wheel. The player's actions should drive the story.

*Influence through initial conditions.* Narrative features should generally be established at the start of play for all players to see. Mid-game narrative events are delicate — they're more acceptable when they affect all units equally (a change in the game world) than when they arbitrarily affect one unit (a deus ex machina).

*Leave space for the player.* Don't crowd out narrative space. Leave gaps in the backstory that the players' avatars can step into. Make the game's setting about the players' clever actions, not the designer's clever story.

*Be bold.* If you want players to experience a dramatic last stand, don't make the defender slightly outnumbered — make them vastly outnumbered and entirely surrounded.

## N §4 — Emergent Narrative

Emergent narrative arises from the interactions between the systems provided by your game. It cannot be controlled by the designer, but it absolutely can be designed for.

**Why it matters:** Emergent narrative creates player ownership. If a story arises from the players' own decisions, they feel it belongs to them in a way that embedded narrative never can.

**The guilt example:** Other art forms can make you feel anger at a betrayal. Only games can make you feel *guilt* over the same event, because the players were responsible for it.

---

## N §4.1 — Connect Events

Events create narrative when they are linked in chains. If damaging a unit simply removes it, that's an isolated event. If damaging it causes it to interact with nearby units (cry for help, cause panic, leave resources), chains of causality emerge that players interpret as story.

**Design for:** outputs from one system that become inputs to another.

---

## N §4.2 — Correlate Events

Events do not need to be mechanically connected to create narrative. Players apply "definite and obvious deductive generalisation when any separate objects are placed before us side by side" (Eisenstein).

**Case study — SSO (Ford, 2018):** Players struggle against a malicious AI on a stricken spaceship. External systems fail (driving players outside) and the airlock fails (trapping players outside) — two entirely independent systems. Players consistently invent a narrative of the AI tricking them outside so it could lock them out.

**Design for:** events that share location and type; players will build narrative connections even where none exist mechanically.

---

## N §4.3 — Uncertainty

Uncertainty "infuses a game with dramatic tension" and creates variety in emergent stories. Without it, chains of events become predictable — their artifice becomes obvious.

**Design for:** resolution systems where the same initial conditions can produce different outcomes. Each run of the system tells a different story.

---

## N §4.4 — The Gaslands Case Study (Emergent Chaos)

In Gaslands (Hutchinson, 2017), an extended chain connects:
- Attack roll → hit → hazard tokens → six tokens = spin out → dice roll → possible flip → hull point loss → possible destruction → dice roll → possible explosion

No step in this chain is inevitable. Many grenades are thrown and produce nothing dramatic. But when the full chain fires, players see a clear emergent narrative of escalating cause and effect arising directly from their own actions.

**Design principle:** Chain reactions should be:
- Possible but not probable on any given trigger
- Each step visible and comprehensible to all players
- Each step interactable (players can potentially interrupt or respond)
- The result, when it occurs, undeniably spectacular

→ Cross-load D §10: activation and narrative — same principle of timing for dramatic beats.
→ Cross-load A §2.3: the scene-sequel loop — each step in the Gaslands chain is a micro-scene.

## N §4.5 — Tips for Emergent Narrative

**Make things act the way players expect.** A weapon described as a grenade must behave like a grenade — area effects, thrown trajectory, possibility of being thrown back. If mechanical behaviour matches conceptual expectations, events slot into player narrative automatically.

**Leave gaps.** "If a player's imagination needs to cross a gap, the player will own the gap" (Kennedy). But: creating a story from a pile of randomly discovered pictures is bad. There should be clarity and intention in your gaps.

**Open to interpretation.** Events expressed generically invite player interpretation. The Warhammer 40,000 "valuable relic" scenario deliberately avoids specifying what the relic is — players integrate it into whatever narrative they've built. Concrete specifics that contradict the player's vision stall narrative emergence.

## N §5 — Dramatic Focus

Ford and Hutchinson's concept of dramatic focus: the perspective and narrative priority that a game adopts, which determines how players experience events.

### N §5.1 — Objective vs Subjective Viewpoint

**Objective viewpoint:** The game attempts to recreate the causal reality of events. Players approach this reality with their own emotions and interpretations. Every step in the chain of cause-and-effect is modelled. Example: historical simulation games, where accuracy of outcome is the primary goal.

**Subjective viewpoint:** The game is concerned with hitting the moments that tell the story and create the emotions the designer wants. It skips steps it designates unimportant. Example: narrative games where the designer has decided which moments matter.

**Practical impact:** An objective game includes every intermediate step (the bullet must travel, hit armour, penetrate, wound). A subjective game may collapse this to "roll to wound" because the intermediate steps are not dramatically significant.

### N §5.2 — Dramatic Focus and Mechanics

Game mechanics draw the player's attention and create dramatic focus. Whatever the mechanics emphasise will be experienced as the most important events in the game.

**Test:** What do players spend the most time doing during your game? That activity is your game's actual dramatic focus — regardless of what the rules say the focus is. If the activity players spend most time on is not the most fun part, redesign until it is.

→ Cross-load A §5: the dramatic focus of each session stated as one sentence.
→ Cross-load C §6: scenario design — the "why engage?" test is a test of dramatic focus alignment.

## N §6 — Conflict as Narrative Fuel

Every narrative has conflict at its core: someone wants or needs something, but someone or something stands in the way.

Mechanical conflicts (victory conditions, resolution systems) express this structurally, but they need to be framed and contextualised by conceits and backstory to become narrative.

**Design requirement:** Be clear about the nature of the core conflict in the game world, how the players' forces are involved, and what the stakes are. This gives players a lens through which to understand their avatars' motivations, enabling behaviour congruent with those motivations.

**The dissonance trap:** If the story you tell requires faction A to defend their sacred mountain, but the victory conditions make it optimal to abandon the mountain and control peripheral objectives, players will feel the dissonance. Their mechanical incentives and their narrative context are in conflict.

→ Cross-load C §6: victory conditions as signposts to the game's best content.
→ Cross-load I §3.2: the war rationale must be reflected in what the game actually incentivises.

## N §7 — Narrative Design Checklist

**Conceits**
- [ ] Is there a single, clear "You are…" overall conceit?
- [ ] Is it communicated early and loudly in the rules?
- [ ] Do minor conceits throughout the rules connect mechanical abstractions to the game world?

**Embedded Narrative**
- [ ] Does the game provide context without taking control?
- [ ] Is there space left for players to step into?
- [ ] Are any pre-announced mid-game narrative events world-affecting rather than unit-specific?

**Emergent Narrative**
- [ ] Does the resolution system connect events in visible chains?
- [ ] Are there systems that correlate without connecting, allowing player-invented causality?
- [ ] Is there enough uncertainty in each step to create variety?
- [ ] Do things behave the way players expect them to?

**Dramatic Focus**
- [ ] Have you decided whether your game has an objective or subjective viewpoint?
- [ ] Do your mechanics emphasise the same moments your narrative frames as important?
- [ ] Is the most common player activity also the most fun?
